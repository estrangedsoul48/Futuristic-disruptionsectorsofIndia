Executive Summary & Key Findings

India's semiconductor journey has crossed its first inflection point. The era of policy announcements is over. The era of silicon is beginning. This report answers the only question that matters to long-term investors: where will the majority of shareholder wealth actually be created?

The Central Thesis

India will not build the world's most advanced chips in the next decade. It will not challenge TSMC at 3nm. But it will build an increasingly important node in the global semiconductor supply chain — and the companies that own the infrastructure, materials, gases, and services that every fab must consume regardless of which chipmaker wins will compound far more reliably than the chip manufacturers themselves. India's ASML equivalent will not make a lithography machine. It will supply the ultra-pure water, specialty fluorochemicals, power conditioning, or vacuum technology that every fab must have. Find those businesses. That is where wealth compounds.

The Five Most Important Findings

1. ATMP is India's actual leverage point, not fabs. Global semiconductor architecture is shifting from monolithic chips to heterogeneous integration — where chiplets from different manufacturers are assembled into a single package. This makes advanced packaging MORE strategically valuable than wafer fabrication for latecomers. India has correctly identified ATMP (Assembly, Test, Marking, Packaging) as its entry point, and the Micron, CG Semi, and Kaynes Semicon facilities now operational at Sanand represent a genuine, not theatrical, start.

2. The tollbooth businesses are the most durable investments. Every semiconductor fab in India — current and future — requires: electronic-grade specialty gases (ultra-high purity nitrogen, argon, specialty etchants), ultra-pure water at volumes of 1,000–2,000 liters per minute, stable power with no fluctuations beyond 1% of rated voltage, specialty fluoropolymers for chemical delivery, and precision environmental control. These needs are non-negotiable and non-importable. Linde India, Gujarat Fluorochemicals, Navin Fluorine, Va Tech Wabag, ABB India, and Hitachi Energy India are India's most defensible semiconductor toll-booth businesses. Most are almost entirely overlooked in semiconductor-themed investment analysis.

3. India's design talent is its most undermonetized asset. India has approximately 125,000 semiconductor design engineers — roughly 20% of the global pool. Forty of the top 50 global semiconductor companies have design centers in India. The DLI (Design Linked Incentive) scheme is beginning to catalyze genuinely Indian fabless ambitions. Tata Elxsi, Cyient, and L&T Technology Services are best positioned to capture this as full-stack design houses, not just offshore cost centres.

4. ISM 2.0 signals the more important battle. The Indian government's announcement of ISM 2.0 in the Union Budget 2026-27, with its deliberate focus on equipment, materials, design IP, and supply chains rather than more fab announcements, signals sophisticated policy thinking. The government understands that owning the infrastructure behind the fab — the "picks and shovels" — is the real strategic prize. This should guide investors toward materials and equipment businesses over pure-play ATMP operators.

5. Most "semiconductor stocks" the market is pricing are not semiconductor bottleneck businesses. Dixon Technologies, Avalon Technologies, and standard EMS providers are valued as semiconductor plays but face commodity economics, China competition, and thin margins. The genuine moat businesses — specialty fluorochemicals, ultra-pure water, precision vacuum equipment — are still being priced as unrelated industrials. This mispricing is the opportunity.

Part 01

Global Semiconductor Ecosystem Map

The semiconductor industry is not one business — it is a precisely ordered stack of interdependent monopolies, oligopolies, and competitive commodity layers. Understanding which layer you are investing in is the single most important analytical act.

Layer 1: Electronic Design Automation (EDA) — The Invisible Gatekeeper

Before a single chip is fabricated, every transistor, wire, and logic gate must be designed, verified, and simulated in software. This is the domain of Electronic Design Automation. The EDA market is a functional duopoly between Synopsys and Cadence Design Systems, with Siemens EDA (formerly Mentor Graphics) a distant third. These three companies collectively control the tools through which the entirety of modern semiconductor design flows.

EDA is arguably the highest-quality business in the entire technology sector. Gross margins exceed 80%. Switching costs are existential — redesigning a major chip using different EDA tools represents a multi-year, multi-hundred-million-dollar undertaking. Customer relationships persist for decades. The qualification cycle is not months but years. Revenue is predominantly subscription-based with high renewal rates. ROIC regularly exceeds 40%.

Barrier Anatomy: Why EDA Is Irreplaceable

Engineering teams at semiconductor companies spend 5–10 years developing processes and methodologies specific to particular EDA tool suites. The institutional knowledge embedded in these workflows — library characterization, timing constraints, place-and-route recipes — cannot be transferred to competing tools without catastrophic disruption. This is the deepest switching cost in technology.

Layer 2: Semiconductor IP Cores

Most modern chips are not built from scratch. They incorporate licensed IP blocks — processor cores, memory interfaces, USB controllers, GPU engines — that are designed once and licensed to many. Arm Holdings is the defining example: its CPU instruction set architecture underlies virtually every smartphone processor on earth. MIPS Technologies, RISC-V Foundation contributors, and scores of specialized IP vendors populate this layer.

IP licensing generates extraordinarily high margins (60–80% EBITDA) with near-zero incremental cost per unit shipped. The business model — design once, license forever — is structurally superior to almost any other in technology. The primary risk is architectural obsolescence, which occurs on decade-long timescales.

Layer 3: Fabless Design — The Leverage Play

Fabless companies design chips but own no manufacturing capacity. They contract wafer production to foundries. This model concentrates value in intellectual property and customer relationships while outsourcing capital-intensive manufacturing. NVIDIA, Qualcomm, AMD, MediaTek, and Apple (for its custom silicon) are the canonical examples.

The economics of fabless design are exceptional when IP is proprietary: NVIDIA's gross margins exceeded 74% in FY2024, driven by architectural leadership in AI accelerators. However, fabless companies without genuine IP differentiation (commodity mobile chips, standard controllers) face brutal pricing pressure as foundry access democratizes.

Layer 4: Semiconductor Foundries

Foundries — notably TSMC, Samsung Foundry, GlobalFoundries, and UMC — manufacture chips on behalf of fabless designers. Leading-edge foundry (3nm, 2nm nodes) is extraordinarily capital intensive: a single modern fab costs $20–30 billion and requires 3–5 years to construct. TSMC's capital expenditure in 2024 was approximately $30 billion — greater than many national defense budgets.

The foundry business is bifurcated. Leading-edge foundry (sub-7nm) is dominated by TSMC with near-absolute pricing power and 55%+ gross margins. Trailing-edge foundry (28nm, 40nm, 65nm) is increasingly competitive, with Chinese players (SMIC) adding capacity aggressively, driving margin compression for all trailing-edge manufacturers.

India's Foundry Position: The Tata Electronics-PSMC fab at Dholera, Gujarat — India's first commercial silicon fab — targets 28nm and more mature nodes. This is a strategic entry point, not a leading-edge play. Realistic assessment: India will be a meaningful 28–65nm fab location by 2028–2030, serving domestic demand and potentially some export market in mature-node products (automotive, industrial, power management chips).

Layer 5: Semiconductor Equipment — The Hidden Masters

No fab can operate without the equipment that deposits, etches, implants, measures, and inspects layers of material on silicon wafers. This equipment layer is where some of the highest-quality businesses in the industrial world reside. ASML (lithography), Applied Materials (deposition/etch), Lam Research (etch/deposition), KLA Corporation (inspection/metrology), and Tokyo Electron (process equipment) collectively represent approximately $150 billion in annual revenue with gross margins of 45–55% and exceptional pricing power.

Semiconductor equipment businesses benefit from what might be called the "three lock-ins": process lock-in (the specific recipe for using a particular machine in a particular process cannot easily transfer to a competitor's machine), service lock-in (complex equipment requires trained field service engineers whose institutional knowledge is embedded in the installed base), and qualification lock-in (any new equipment must be re-qualified at each customer before it can be used in production — a process that takes 12–24 months).

Layer 6: Semiconductor Materials

A modern semiconductor fab consumes hundreds of specialty chemicals, gases, and materials in its manufacturing process. These include photoresists (light-sensitive polymers used in photolithography), electronic-grade ultra-pure chemicals (hydrofluoric acid, sulfuric acid, hydrogen peroxide), specialty process gases (NF3, WF6, SiH4, HCl), silicon wafers, CMP slurries (abrasive compounds for polishing), and specialty films (tantalum nitride, titanium nitride).

The materials layer is characterized by extreme purity requirements. Electronic-grade hydrofluoric acid, for instance, requires purity of 99.9999%+ — impurities at parts-per-billion levels can destroy chip yield. This creates significant technical barriers for new entrants and justifies premium pricing. Gross margins in specialty semiconductor materials range from 35–60% depending on specificity and purity requirements.

India's Materials Opportunity

India's specialty chemicals industry — anchored by Gujarat Fluorochemicals, Navin Fluorine, SRF Limited, and Aether Industries — has the foundational chemistry capabilities to serve the semiconductor materials market. The gap is in achieving semiconductor-grade purity levels and obtaining the customer qualifications that typically take 18–36 months. Companies making this investment now will own a critical bottleneck when India's fab ecosystem reaches full operation.

Layer 7: Advanced Packaging & OSAT

After wafers are fabricated, chips must be assembled, packaged, and tested before shipment. OSAT (Outsourced Semiconductor Assembly and Test) has historically been considered a low-value, labor-intensive step in the supply chain. That calculus is changing fundamentally. The shift to heterogeneous integration — assembling multiple chiplets from different processes and manufacturers into a single package — has made advanced packaging a strategic capability, not a commodity service.

TSMC's CoWoS (Chip-on-Wafer-on-Substrate) packaging technology, used in NVIDIA's AI GPUs, has become a genuine bottleneck limiting AI chip supply. Advanced packaging now commands margins of 25–35% in gross terms, with leaders like TSMC Advanced Packaging achieving 40%+. Traditional OSAT (wire bonding, standard BGA packaging) operates at 15–22% gross margins with ongoing margin pressure.

This layer is India's primary current opportunity. The operational ATMP facilities at Sanand (Micron, CG Semi, Kaynes Semicon) represent India's first credible claim to a role in the global semiconductor supply chain. Advanced packaging capabilities must now be the stated strategic goal for ISM Phase 2.

Layer 8: Testing & Inspection

Semiconductor testing occurs at multiple stages: wafer-level test, die-level test, and package-level final test. Testing equipment from Teradyne, Advantest, and Cohu represents sophisticated capital equipment with high gross margins (50–60% for the test equipment manufacturers). In India, this layer is nascent but critical — companies like Data Patterns (defense/aerospace testing systems), Astra Microwave, and Centum Electronics have relevant capabilities that could expand into semiconductor testing adjacent applications.

Layer 9: Sub-Fab Infrastructure — The True Tollbooth

The most overlooked layer in semiconductor analysis is the sub-fab — the infrastructure beneath and around the cleanroom that makes chip manufacturing physically possible. This includes: ultra-pure water (UPW) systems generating 18.2 megaohm-cm resistivity water at volumes of 1,500–2,000 liters/minute per fab; bulk gas distribution for nitrogen, argon, hydrogen, and oxygen; specialty gas supply (NF3, HCl, specialty etchants) in safe storage and delivery systems; power supply with uninterruptible, ultra-clean power (semiconductor fabs require voltage stability within 1%, with microsecond-level response times); HVAC/environmental control maintaining cleanrooms at ±0.1°C and humidity within ±1%; and industrial waste treatment for the enormous quantities of hazardous effluent generated.

This is the layer where India's most underappreciated semiconductor investments reside. Companies providing UPW, power quality, industrial gases, and environmental control will serve every fab India builds, regardless of which chip company wins.

Part 02

Anatomy of a Bottleneck Business

The most valuable semiconductor businesses share a specific anatomy. They are not simply large or profitable — they own a structural position in the value chain that competitors cannot easily replicate, and from which they extract rents that grow as the industry grows. Understanding this anatomy is the analytical foundation for finding India's equivalents.

The ASML Blueprint: How a Monopoly Is Built

ASML's extreme ultraviolet (EUV) lithography machine — the most complex manufacturing tool ever created — represents the ultimate expression of bottleneck business construction. Each EUV machine contains approximately 100,000 components sourced from over 5,000 global suppliers, produces a beam of light at 13.5nm wavelength, and performs in the equivalent of a scientific instrument that must operate at production-line reliability in a semiconductor cleanroom.

ASML did not achieve this position through superior marketing. It achieved it through 30+ years of compounding R&D investment, the accumulation of irreplaceable institutional knowledge in its engineering teams, the development of an ecosystem of suppliers and service engineers whose capabilities are co-created and co-dependent, and the establishment of qualification relationships with every major leading-edge foundry that took decades to develop. The economic consequences are severe: ASML's gross margin regularly exceeds 50%, its EBITDA margin exceeds 32%, and its ROIC exceeds 30%. No customer can switch. No competitor can replicate. The price of each EUV machine — approximately $180 million — increases with each generation, and customers queue for years to receive delivery.

The ASML model teaches us this: the most durable competitive positions in semiconductors are built through decades of compounding technical complexity, the development of co-dependencies with customers that make switching existentially risky, and the creation of service and support networks that are as valuable as the hardware itself.

TSMC: Owning the Factory Nobody Else Can Build

TSMC's moat has three dimensions. First, process knowledge: TSMC's manufacturing recipes — the precise sequences of deposition, etch, and implantation steps that produce functional 3nm chips — represent the accumulated result of thousands of engineer-years of iterative refinement. This knowledge lives in the minds of TSMC's engineers, in the algorithms of its process control systems, and in the specific characteristics of its installed equipment base. It cannot be downloaded or purchased.

Second, the ecosystem lock-in: TSMC's process design kits (PDKs) have been used by thousands of chip design teams to design tens of thousands of products. These designs are validated against TSMC's specific process, and moving them to another foundry requires re-design, re-verification, and re-qualification. The accumulated inertia of the fabless ecosystem anchors demand to TSMC permanently.

Third, scale: TSMC's leading-edge R&D spending of $5+ billion annually is supportable because it is amortized across revenues that no competitor can match. This creates a permanent widening of the technology gap.

Six Common Traits of Semiconductor Bottleneck Businesses

Analysis of ASML, TSMC, Synopsys, KLA, and other dominant semiconductor businesses reveals six common structural characteristics. First, technology complexity that is exponential in accumulation — each year of R&D compounds upon the last, making the gap between leader and follower grow wider over time, not narrower. Second, qualification-driven lock-in — the 12–24 month process of validating a new product or supplier in a semiconductor production line creates extraordinary switching costs and a structurally recurring revenue stream once qualified. Third, service ecosystem moats — the installed base of complex equipment generates a service and spare parts revenue stream that is higher-margin than original equipment sales and creates continuous customer engagement. Fourth, co-development relationships — leading semiconductor companies develop their next-generation technology in partnership with key suppliers, embedding those suppliers into the roadmap and making competitive displacement prohibitively expensive. Fifth, network effects in standards and ecosystems — EDA tools, IP libraries, and foundry PDKs exhibit strong network effects where the value of the platform increases as more users adopt it. Sixth, pricing power divorced from cost — the most critical semiconductor inputs are priced based on the value they deliver, not the cost to produce them. A $180 million EUV machine is not priced as $150 million of metal and optics plus margin — it is priced as the enabling technology of a $1 trillion industry.

Part 03

India's Semiconductor Ambition

India's semiconductor policy has evolved from aspiration to action. Understanding where the policy is genuinely strong, where it is still aspirational, and where the investment community has drawn incorrect conclusions is essential for accurate capital allocation.

ISM 1.0: The Foundation (2021–2025)

India Semiconductor Mission 1.0, approved in December 2021 with an incentive outlay of ₹76,000 crore, established the policy architecture for semiconductor development in India. The scheme offered up to 50% fiscal support on project costs for silicon fabs and 50% on capital expenditure for compound semiconductor fabs and ATMP/OSAT facilities. A Design Linked Incentive (DLI) scheme ran concurrently to encourage indigenous chip design.

As of December 2025, ten projects had been approved across six states representing committed investments of ₹1.60 lakh crore (~$19 billion). The milestones achieved under ISM 1.0 are genuinely significant: Micron's ATMP facility in Sanand achieved commercial production; CG Semi's OSAT launched pilot production in August 2025 as India's first full-service OSAT provider offering traditional and advanced packaging; and Kaynes Semicon shipped its first commercial chips in October 2025. The Tata Electronics-PSMC wafer fab in Dholera is under active construction, targeting operation by 2027.

ISM 2.0: The Smarter Battle (2026 Onwards)

India Semiconductor Mission 2.0, announced in the Union Budget 2026-27, represents a significant maturation of policy thinking. Where ISM 1.0 focused on attracting manufacturing, ISM 2.0 targets the deeper supply chain: semiconductor equipment and materials, full-stack Indian semiconductor IP development, supply chain localization, and industry-led research and training centers.

An additional ₹8,000 crore financial outlay for 2026-27 under the Modified Programme for Development of Semiconductor and Display Manufacturing Ecosystem signals genuine financial commitment. The explicit focus on equipment and materials is particularly important — it reflects a recognition that India's semiconductor ambitions cannot be realized with a 90%+ import dependency in critical process inputs.

Policy Ambition vs. Ground Reality

What India Cannot Realistically Achieve by 2030

India will not manufacture leading-edge chips (sub-7nm) within the next decade under any realistic scenario. The capital requirements, technology gaps, and talent depth for leading-edge nodes represent a 25-year head start that Taiwan and South Korea have built through sustained national focus. India's stated goal of "3nm and 2nm manufacturing capabilities by 2035" is aspirationally stated, not engineered. Investors who price Indian semiconductor stocks on leading-edge fab assumptions are making a category error.

What India can realistically achieve: By 2030, India can credibly establish itself as a global ATMP center for mature-node chips, operate India's first commercial silicon fab at 28–65nm nodes (serving automotive, industrial, and power management markets), develop a meaningful compound semiconductor cluster (GaN, SiC for power and RF), and begin building a domestic supply chain in materials and gases that currently requires 100% importation. By 2035, a second fab wave is plausible, advanced packaging capabilities competitive with Southeast Asia are achievable, and the design ecosystem can produce genuinely global fabless companies.

The investor misconception to avoid: Most "India semiconductor" themed investing focuses on companies that will manufacture chips. The actual investment returns will concentrate disproportionately in the companies that manufacture the inputs to chip manufacturing — the gases, chemicals, precision equipment, power systems, and infrastructure that every fab must have but that very few investment frameworks currently identify as semiconductor investments.

Part 04

India's Complete Semiconductor Value Chain

A structured view of India's semiconductor value chain reveals significant gaps alongside genuine competitive strengths. Investors must map every tier to find the actual bottleneck opportunities.

Tier 1
Chip Design & IP Ecosystem

India's single greatest semiconductor competitive advantage is its design talent pool. With approximately 125,000 semiconductor design engineers — representing roughly 20% of the world's total — and design centers from 40 of the top 50 semiconductor companies, India is already deeply embedded in the global chip design supply chain. The question is not whether India can design chips, but whether India can capture the full value of its design capabilities rather than functioning as a cost-arbitrage destination for foreign semiconductor companies.

The Design Linked Incentive (DLI) scheme provides financial support to Indian companies across multiple stages: product design (₹15 crore over 3 years), commercialization (20% of project cost over 4 years), and deployment support. Early beneficiaries include multiple fabless startups in the automotive, IoT, and industrial domains. Signalchip Innovations (radar chipsets), Saankhya Labs (broadcast semiconductors), and InCore Semiconductors (RISC-V based SoCs) represent India's nascent but promising fabless ecosystem.

The listed design-adjacent universe — Tata Elxsi, L&T Technology Services, Cyient — increasingly operates as integrated design engineering firms rather than pure services businesses, developing and retaining more IP. This trajectory, if maintained, could produce genuine IP licensing businesses within a decade.

Tier 2
ATMP / OSAT

India's ATMP ecosystem is real, active, and growing. The Sanand cluster in Gujarat has emerged as India's first genuine semiconductor manufacturing cluster, housing Micron, CG Semi, and Kaynes Semicon within a geographic proximity that enables supply chain development. The strategic importance of ATMP cannot be overstated in the current technology environment: as AI drives demand for heterogeneous chip architectures that combine multiple chiplets in a single package, assembly and packaging expertise becomes increasingly strategic.

The key differentiator for Indian ATMP players will be whether they move up the value stack from traditional wire-bonding and BGA packaging toward advanced packaging technologies — flip chip, fan-out wafer-level packaging (FOWLP), 2.5D and 3D integration. CG Semi's partnership with Renesas (which has deep advanced packaging expertise) and Kaynes Semicon's engineering-driven approach are positive indicators. The companies that qualify for advanced packaging — not just standard OSAT — will command significantly higher margins and face dramatically lower competition from Chinese players.

Tier 3
Semiconductor Equipment Ecosystem

India's equipment manufacturing capability for semiconductor applications is at an early embryonic stage, with several exceptions. ASM Technologies (NSE: ASMTEC), through its JV with HHV Group (ASM-HHV Engineering), has established India's first dedicated semiconductor equipment manufacturing facility, producing tools, sub-systems, and components for the semiconductor and solar industries. This is a genuinely significant milestone that is entirely overlooked by mainstream semiconductor investment analysis in India.

The broader precision engineering ecosystem — anchored by firms such as MTAR Technologies, Jyoti CNC Automation, and Bharat Fritz Werner — has relevant CNC machining and precision fabrication capabilities that can be redirected toward semiconductor equipment components. Semiconductor equipment demands tolerances in the micron to sub-micron range, which is achievable with India's better precision engineering firms.

However, the technology gap in semiconductor-specific equipment — CVD systems, etch reactors, ion implantation equipment, advanced metrology tools — is severe. India cannot realistically manufacture these domestically within a 10-year horizon without substantial technology transfer partnerships. The realistic ISM 2.0 equipment opportunity is in sub-components, fixtures, and service parts for globally manufactured equipment, not the equipment itself.

The exception worth watching: HHV Group's vacuum technology capabilities are world-class for a company of its size. The ASM-HHV joint venture is the closest India has to an embryonic semiconductor equipment company. Investors interested in this space should monitor ASM Technologies closely as the vehicle for accessing this private-company capability.

Tier 4
Semiconductor Materials

India's specialty chemicals industry is larger, more sophisticated, and more relevant to semiconductor manufacturing than most investors appreciate. The fluorochemicals complex — anchored by Gujarat Fluorochemicals (INOXGFL Group), Navin Fluorine International, and SRF Limited — produces fundamental building-block chemicals that are directly or indirectly used in semiconductor processing.

The specific opportunities are as follows. Gujarat Fluorochemicals is actively developing FFKM (perfluoroelastomer) grades specifically for semiconductor applications at its Dahej-A facility, and has developed ultra-high purity PFA (perfluoroalkoxy) grades tailored for semiconductor-grade tubing and fittings used in chemical delivery systems within fabs. Navin Fluorine is evaluating production of electronic-grade hydrofluoric acid (HF), a critical etching chemical currently imported in semiconductor-grade purity. SRF Limited's fluorochemicals division produces specialty compounds with semiconductor material adjacency. Aether Industries, producing high-value specialty chemicals at industrial scale, has relevant chemistry capabilities for semiconductor intermediate production.

The challenge in all cases is the semiconductor-grade purity barrier. Electronic materials must typically achieve 99.999% or greater purity with specific impurity profiles. This requires dedicated manufacturing infrastructure, highly stringent quality systems, and extended customer qualification cycles. The companies that invest in these capabilities now — while the Indian fab ecosystem is still nascent — will be the qualified suppliers when demand reaches critical mass around 2028–2030.

One critical gap: India has no domestic manufacturer of photoresists, the light-sensitive polymers central to photolithography. The photoresist market is dominated by JSR, Tokyo Ohka Kogyo, Shin-Etsu Chemical, and DuPont. This represents a strategic gap and a long-term opportunity for advanced materials companies willing to invest in the 5–10 year development cycle required.

Tier 5
Semiconductor Infrastructure

The infrastructure layer is India's most mature and most underappreciated semiconductor investment opportunity. Every fab — regardless of technology node, chip type, or ownership — requires identical critical infrastructure: clean power, pure water, controlled environment, and specialty gas supply. India has competent, listed industrial companies in every one of these categories.

Ultra-Pure Water: Va Tech Wabag (NSE: WABAG) designs, builds, and operates water and wastewater treatment infrastructure including UPW systems. A 300mm fab consuming 2,000 L/min of UPW generates a multi-decade operational relationship for the UPW system designer and operator. Wabag's existing relationships with industrial clients and growing semiconductor-specific expertise make it the most direct India-listed play on fab water infrastructure.

Power Quality: ABB India (NSE: ABB), Siemens India (NSE: SIEMENS), and Hitachi Energy India (NSE: POWERINFA) supply power transformers, UPS systems, and power quality conditioning equipment. A semiconductor fab's power reliability requirements are among the most demanding of any industrial facility — a single voltage transient lasting one millisecond can destroy an entire lot of wafers worth tens of millions of dollars. The companies that supply, install, and service power conditioning infrastructure for India's fabs will have long-term, high-value service relationships.

Industrial & Specialty Gases: Linde India (NSE: LINDEINDIA) is India's incumbent industrial gases leader with the technical capabilities and safety certifications to supply electronic-grade gases. Inox Air Products (INOXAP) is expanding aggressively. Specialty semiconductor gases (NF3 for chamber cleaning, WF6 for tungsten deposition, specialty etchants) must be supplied domestically — these gases cannot be transported internationally in the required purity and at the required volumes. This geographic necessity gives gas suppliers a captive market within each fab cluster.

HVAC and Cooling: Thermax (NSE: THERMAX) and Blue Star (NSE: BLUESTAR) supply industrial HVAC and process cooling systems. Semiconductor cleanrooms must maintain temperature stability within ±0.1°C and humidity within ±1%, 24 hours a day, 365 days a year. This creates multi-decade service and maintenance contracts.

Part 05

Where Value Lives — The India Margin & ROIC Map

Not all semiconductor exposure is created equal. The margin and ROIC profiles of different positions in India's semiconductor value chain vary by factors of five to ten. Investors must rank these opportunities rather than treating all "semiconductor exposure" as equivalent.

Tier A: Highest-Quality Positions (Own Forever)

The highest-quality investment positions in India's semiconductor ecosystem combine three characteristics: non-importable necessity (the input cannot be sourced internationally at competitive cost or with adequate reliability), high qualification barriers (once qualified as a supplier, the switching cost for the customer is prohibitive), and compounding demand (the customer base grows with India's semiconductor ambitions regardless of which specific chip company wins).

In India, electronic-grade specialty gases and chemicals meet all three criteria. Semiconductor-grade gases (ultra-high purity nitrogen, argon, specialty etchants) cannot be imported into a fab at the volumes and purity levels required — they must be produced at or near the fab site. Electronic-grade hydrofluoric acid and specialty fluoropolymers can be sourced internationally, but domestic production eliminates supply chain risk and provides cost advantages. The companies investing in semiconductor-grade capabilities today — Linde India in gases, Gujarat Fluorochemicals and Navin Fluorine in specialty fluorochemicals — will own qualified supplier positions for 10–20 years once established. Ultra-pure water infrastructure similarly creates multi-decade service relationships. Power quality infrastructure for fabs creates 15–25 year installed base relationships.

Tier B: Attractive Positions (Own During Build-Out)

Advanced ATMP/OSAT — specifically players building genuine advanced packaging capabilities rather than commodity wire bonding — represents an attractive medium-term investment with growing moat potential. CG Power (via CG Semi with Renesas expertise) and Kaynes Semicon have credible advanced packaging trajectories. Precision engineering firms qualifying for semiconductor tool components (MTAR Technologies, Centum Electronics) similarly represent durable, growing positions once technical qualification is achieved.

Chip design services (Tata Elxsi, L&T Technology Services, Cyient) represent high-margin, export-oriented businesses with growing IP content. As these firms transition from hourly engineering services to project-based and IP-royalty models, their economics will improve structurally.

Tier C: Cyclical but Investable (Trade Around the Cycle)

Standard OSAT — commodity assembly and test of mature-node chips — is a good business during India's capacity-building phase but will face increasing pricing pressure from Chinese OSAT players (OSATs are not subject to US export controls) as Indian capacity matures. The ASML Framework — Applied to India

The "ASML test" asks a simple but brutal question: if this company disappeared tomorrow, would semiconductor manufacturing in India halt? How difficult would replacement be? How long would re-qualification take? The answers reveal which companies own genuine bottleneck positions and which merely exist in the same ecosystem.

ASML Score Methodology

Each company is scored 0–10 across four dimensions: (1) Criticality — how essential is the company's product/service to semiconductor manufacturing? (2) Technology Moat — how deep is the technical barrier to entry? (3) Replication Difficulty — how hard would it be for a new entrant to replicate the capability? (4) Customer Dependence — what is the switching cost for semiconductor customers? The composite score weights criticality at 40%, replication difficulty at 30%, technology moat at 20%, and customer dependence at 10%.

Company Deep Dives

Detailed analysis of eight companies representing the spectrum of investment quality in India's semiconductor ecosystem.

CG Power & Industrial Solutions (NSE: CGPOWER)

Why the market misses it: CG Power is primarily covered as an electrical equipment company (transformers, motors) with a recent semiconductor venture. The semiconductor element (CG Semi) is treated as a small, unproven subsidiary rather than as a potentially transformative business unit.

Bottleneck solved: CG Semi — the OSAT joint venture with Japan's Renesas Electronics and Thailand's Stars Microelectronics — became India's first full-service OSAT provider offering both traditional and advanced packaging technologies when its pilot line launched in August 2025. The Renesas partnership is the critical differentiator: Renesas brings decades of advanced packaging expertise and, critically, a ready customer pipeline in automotive and industrial semiconductors. This is not a speculative venture — it is a technology transfer with a committed anchor customer.

Technology differentiation: The Renesas partnership provides access to advanced OSAT processes that India's ATMP ecosystem would otherwise require a decade to develop organically. Wire bonding, flip chip, and advanced package-on-package capabilities are being transferred, not re-invented.

10-year opportunity: If CG Semi successfully qualifies for advanced packaging by 2027–2028, it occupies a position in India's OSAT ecosystem that is extremely difficult to displace. Combined with CG Power's established industrial moat (transformers, motors, drives), the company offers a genuine combination of short-term industrial compounding and long-term semiconductor optionality. Bear case: advanced packaging qualification is harder than anticipated; China-based OSAT competition intensifies; margins in standard OSAT compress to commodity levels. Bull case: India's ATMP ecosystem reaches critical mass by 2029, CG Semi is the incumbent advanced packaging provider, and Renesas drives global order flow into the facility.

Gujarat Fluorochemicals — INOXGFL (NSE: FLUOROCHEM)

Why the market misses it: Covered almost exclusively as a fluorochemicals/refrigerant company with battery materials optionality. The semiconductor materials angle — while explicitly stated in company disclosures — is treated as a distant future development rather than a current investment thesis.

Bottleneck solved: GFL's Dahej-A plant is actively developing FFKM (perfluoroelastomer) grades specifically designed for semiconductor applications. FFKM seals and gaskets are used extensively in semiconductor process equipment at points of contact with aggressive chemicals and extreme temperatures — and must meet purity specifications that GFL is explicitly developing for. Additionally, GFL has developed ultra-high purity PFA (perfluoroalkoxy) grades for semiconductor-grade tubing and fittings used in chemical delivery within fabs. These are not future aspirations — they are active product development programs described in GFL's FY25 annual report.

Margin profile: Specialty fluoropolymers (PTFE, PVDF, PFA, FFKM) command gross margins of 45–60%, significantly above GFL's blended average. Semiconductor-grade product premiums on top of specialty fluoropolymer pricing could add a further 20–30% price realization over standard grades. The total addressable market for electronic fluoropolymers in India's planned semiconductor ecosystem is small today (~₹500–800 crore at peak current capacity) but grows proportionally with fab activity.

Bull case / Bear case: Bull: GFL achieves semiconductor-grade qualifications at multiple fabs, becomes a preferred domestic supplier displacing currently imported materials, and benefits from government sourcing preferences under ISM 2.0's materials focus. Bear: Qualification timelines extend beyond 3 years, the capital cycle in battery chemicals consumes management bandwidth, and the fluoropolymers business faces pricing pressure from Chinese PTFE producers.

Va Tech Wabag (NSE: WABAG)

Why the market misses it: Water treatment is one of the least glamorous sectors in Indian equities. Wabag is analyzed as a project-based infrastructure company with lumpy revenue recognition, not as a potential semiconductor infrastructure toll-booth business.

Bottleneck solved: A 300mm semiconductor wafer fab requires ultra-pure water at approximately 1,500–2,000 liters per minute, 24 hours a day, at a purity of 18.2 MΩ·cm — the theoretical maximum purity of water. This water is used in virtually every wet processing step: cleaning wafers between etch steps, rinsing after chemical mechanical planarization, and preparing chemical solutions. There is no substitute. You cannot purchase UPW from a neighboring city. It must be produced on-site from local water sources using purpose-designed reverse osmosis, ion exchange, UV treatment, and filtration systems. Wabag designs, builds, operates, and maintains exactly these systems. The Micron facility at Sanand — one of the most visible semiconductor projects in India — required exactly this type of industrial water infrastructure.

Recurring revenue model: Beyond the initial construction of UPW systems, Wabag generates recurring revenue from operations and maintenance (O&M) contracts that typically run for 10–20 years. For a semiconductor fab operating 24/7/365, UPW system downtime is catastrophic — the O&M relationship is essentially non-cancellable.

10-year opportunity: Each new fab and major OSAT facility in India requires a UPW system. With 10 projects currently approved and a pipeline extending to 2030–2035, Wabag has a growing, highly specific semiconductor-related addressable market developing under its nose. The market does not price this opportunity at all.

Data Patterns (India) (NSE: DATAPATTNS)

Why the market misses it: Covered primarily as a defense electronics company. The semiconductor dimension — chip-level design, ASIC development, FPGA-based systems — is acknowledged but not analyzed as a standalone bottleneck business.

Bottleneck solved: Data Patterns designs and manufactures integrated defense and aerospace electronic systems — including radar signal processors, avionics systems, and FPGA-based electronic warfare modules — at the chip-level. Once a defense platform is qualified to use Data Patterns' electronics, switching suppliers involves years of re-qualification, re-certification, and re-approval through India's defense procurement processes. This represents one of the deepest moats in Indian electronics manufacturing.

Semiconductor linkage: As India's armed forces upgrade their electronic warfare, radar, and avionics capabilities, the demand for defense-grade semiconductor content (application-specific integrated circuits, FPGAs, hardened memory) flows through companies like Data Patterns that integrate these components. The company's chip-level design capabilities position it to potentially develop proprietary ICs for Indian defense applications — a capability explicitly encouraged by ISM's defense semiconductor programs.

ASML Score 7.6 rationale: In the defense context, Data Patterns approaches the highest ASML scores achievable — it is near-impossible to displace a qualified defense electronics supplier without multi-year disruption to Indian defense programs. The qualification barriers effectively create a permanent installed base.

Linde India (NSE: LINDEINDIA)

Why the market misses it: Industrial gases is an unglamorous sector. Linde India benefits from the Linde global group's semiconductor expertise but is analyzed as a local industrial gas distributor rather than a critical semiconductor infrastructure provider.

The geographic captivity argument: This is possibly the most powerful structural argument for any company in India's semiconductor supply chain. Semiconductor manufacturing requires bulk gases (nitrogen for inert atmosphere, argon for sputtering, hydrogen for reduction) at volumes that cannot be transported economically from a distance. Each major fab requires an on-site or adjacent gas production facility — an Air Separation Unit (ASU) producing nitrogen/oxygen from air, plus distribution infrastructure for specialty gases. The gas supplier who builds this infrastructure and signs the supply contract with a fab typically holds the contract for the operational lifetime of that facility, which is 20–30 years. This is as close to a permanent geographic monopoly as exists in industrial business.

Specialty gas development: Beyond bulk gases, Linde India has the technical infrastructure and safety certifications to supply electronic-grade specialty gases (NF3 for chamber cleaning, WF6 for tungsten deposition, specialty etchants). The development of domestic specialty gas supply, currently almost entirely imported, is a critical element of ISM 2.0's materials focus. Linde India's global parent provides the technology transfer pathway.

Centum Electronics (NSE: CENTUM)

Bottleneck solved: Centum designs and manufactures custom ASICs and hybrid microcircuits for defense, space, and aerospace applications. Centum is one of very few Indian companies with the cleanroom infrastructure, quality systems, and technical capability to produce semiconductor-grade electronics for mission-critical applications.

Its existing cleanroom and precision manufacturing infrastructure is directly transferable to advanced semiconductor packaging applications.

Export potential: Defense-grade electronics from qualified Indian manufacturers serve an increasingly attractive global market. US defense supply chain diversification away from Chinese suppliers creates opportunities for Indian companies with the right certifications and capabilities. Centum has path to becoming a meaningful defense electronics exporter — a position essentially absent from its current market valuation.

Tata Elxsi (NSE: TATAELXSI)

Positioning: Tata Elxsi's semiconductor design practice serves global chip companies needing design engineering, verification, and embedded software development. The company has developed genuine depth in semiconductor IP for automotive (ADAS), broadcast, and industrial applications. Its relationship with Tata Electronics — which is building India's first silicon fab — creates an internal demand pathway for its design services that could become a proprietary competitive advantage as India's domestic chip ecosystem develops.

The IP transition: The most important long-term question for Tata Elxsi is whether it can retain a growing share of the IP it co-develops with global semiconductor companies. Companies that achieve this transition — from service provider to IP licensor — see structural margin improvement that can reach 20+ percentage points in gross margin terms. Tata Elxsi's investment in proprietary platforms (Intelliconnect, AXIS) represents the early stages of this IP accumulation strategy.

Astra Microwave Products (NSE: ASTRAMICRO)

This customer-anchor dynamic has been critical in developing semiconductor ecosystems in other countries — it is likely to be equally important in India.

Part 10

Future Bottleneck Owners

Which Indian companies could become the structural equivalent of ASML, Synopsys, Applied Materials, or TSMC for the Indian and eventually global semiconductor supply chain?

India's ASML: The Vacuum and Deposition Equipment Contender

The most credible candidate for India's closest analog to a semiconductor equipment monopolist is the ASM-HHV Engineering JV (accessible via ASM Technologies, NSE: ASMTEC). HHV Group's 61 years of vacuum technology expertise, combined with ASM Technologies' semiconductor engineering capabilities, has produced the only India-based company with genuine semiconductor equipment manufacturing capability. The JV has already demonstrated the ability to design and produce semiconductor tools and sub-systems. If India's semiconductor ecosystem grows as planned, ASM-HHV has a first-mover advantage in domestic equipment supply that is nearly impossible for a new entrant to replicate quickly. The path from sub-system supplier to full equipment manufacturer is a decade-long journey — but ASM-HHV is the only Indian company on that journey today.

India's Synopsys: The Design Platform Aspiration

India does not currently have a company positioned to become the domestic equivalent of Synopsys or Cadence. EDA software requires decades of compounding algorithmic development, deep integration with global semiconductor foundry processes, and a worldwide customer base that creates self-reinforcing adoption. This is not a 10-year opportunity for an Indian company — it is a 20–30 year journey, if it is achievable at all. However, Tata Elxsi has the potential to become the equivalent of a Synopsys design partner — a company whose methodologies, platforms, and verification flows are deeply embedded in the semiconductor design processes of global chipmakers. This is a more achievable, if less monopolistic, version of the EDA moat.

More realistically, India's "Synopsys equivalent" may emerge in the domain of automotive semiconductor software platforms, where KPIT Technologies is the most credible candidate, or in domain-specific design IP for defense/space applications, where Data Patterns and Centum Electronics are accumulating capability.

India's Applied Materials: The Process Chemistry Path

Applied Materials' dominance derives from its mastery of thin film deposition and etch chemistry. The nearest Indian equivalent path goes through specialty chemicals — specifically the companies developing semiconductor-grade specialty chemicals (GFL, Navin Fluorine) and the vacuum technology companies (ASM-HHV). None of these is close to the Applied Materials role today, but a combination of specialty chemical expertise and process tool manufacturing — potentially under the ISM 2.0 equipment incentive program — could produce a credible 20-year trajectory. The government's explicit focus on semiconductor equipment under ISM 2.0 creates the policy environment for this development if private capital follows.

India's TSMC: The Realistic Assessment

Tata Electronics-PSMC will be India's closest analog to TSMC for at least two decades — and it will operate at mature nodes (28nm and above) rather than the leading edge (3nm and below) where TSMC's irreplaceable value lives. This is not a criticism — it is an accurate description. A mature-node foundry serving India's domestic automotive, industrial, and consumer electronics markets is a commercially viable and strategically important business. It is not a global chip leadership position, but it is a genuine industrial asset with durable demand from India's growing electronics manufacturing base.

The scenario for an Indian company approaching TSMC's strategic position — owning a critical-path capability that no one can replicate — is more plausible in advanced packaging than in wafer fabrication. If an Indian OSAT company (CG Semi, Kaynes Semicon) invests in heterogeneous integration capabilities over the next decade and achieves qualification from global semiconductor companies, it could own a packaging capability that is genuinely difficult to replicate. This is the TSMC-equivalent path that is actually achievable for India within 15–20 years.

India's Linde (Industrial Gases): Already Present

India's "Linde" already exists. Linde India (NSE: LINDEINDIA) IS India's semiconductor gas toll-booth, backed by the Linde global group's technical expertise. Inox Air Products is the fast-growing domestic challenger. The dynamic between these two companies over the next 10 years — as India's semiconductor cluster grows — will determine which company owns the geographic gas monopoly positions around each major fab.

Capital Cycle Analysis

Understanding where capital is flooding, where it remains undersupplied, and which positions are durable vs. cyclical is critical for timing India's semiconductor investment cycle.

Where Capital Is Flooding — Risk of Oversupply

Standard OSAT / Traditional Packaging: Multiple Indian OSAT facilities are being approved simultaneously (CG Semi, Kaynes Semicon, Tata Assam, HCL-Foxconn, Suchi Semicon). Traditional wire-bonding and BGA packaging is a commodity service with Chinese competition from players like Jiangsu Changjiang Electronics Technology (JCET) and Huatian Technology. Capital flooding into standard OSAT creates the risk of near-term oversupply relative to qualified order flow, particularly before India's domestic fab ecosystem generates sufficient wafer output to supply OSAT facilities domestically.

Standard EMS: India's EMS sector is expanding rapidly under PLI, with multiple large greenfield facilities being constructed simultaneously. The beneficiaries of this expansion — mobile phone assembly, consumer electronics — face well-established competitive dynamics and thin margins. Valuations for EMS companies in India are elevated relative to the business quality they represent, driven by the semiconductor narrative rather than the underlying EMS economics.

Where Capital Remains Undersupplied — The Real Opportunity

Electronic-Grade Materials: India currently imports virtually 100% of its semiconductor-grade chemicals, gases, and specialty materials. The ISM 2.0 focus on materials is a recognition of this critical gap. Capital investment in electronic-grade chemical manufacturing in India is at an early stage — the companies investing now (GFL, Navin Fluorine, Linde India, Inox Air Products) face minimal near-term competition for a growing customer base.

Semiconductor Equipment Components: The ISM 2.0 explicitly targets semiconductor equipment manufacturing. The domestic precision engineering ecosystem (Jyoti CNC, MTAR, Bharat Fritz Werner) has the machining capabilities to supply sub-components to semiconductor equipment. Capital for developing semiconductor-specific precision manufacturing has barely begun flowing. The companies that establish semiconductor equipment supply relationships now — when the qualification stakes are lower — will compound those relationships as the ecosystem grows.

Advanced Packaging Technology: While standard OSAT is attracting capital, genuine advanced packaging capability (2.5D, 3D, chiplet integration) is dramatically under-invested in India. The global advanced packaging market is growing at 8–10% annually and commanding significantly higher margins than standard OSAT. The Indian players developing these capabilities (CG Semi with Renesas expertise) are rare and their advanced packaging capacity will be undersupplied relative to growing demand for years.

Sustainable Winners vs. Cyclical Players

Sustainable winners are businesses where competitive position strengthens with time regardless of the semiconductor demand cycle: electronic gas suppliers (geographic captivity), specialty chemical qualifications (once qualified, always preferred), precision engineering expertise (deepens with each project), and advanced packaging technology (compounds with each generation). These businesses should be owned through cycles, not traded around them.

Cyclical winners are businesses that benefit disproportionately during India's semiconductor build phase but face potential margin compression once the initial supply-demand gap closes: standard EMS companies, commodity OSAT operators, and general-purpose infrastructure providers. These merit tactical positioning at cycle troughs rather than long-term holding.

Commodity traps are businesses that carry semiconductor narrative valuations but generate commodity-level returns: pure-play mobile phone assembly, commodity PCB manufacturing, and standard electronic components distribution. These should be avoided at current valuations.

The Road to India's Semiconductor Self-Reliance: 2025–2045

A probabilistic roadmap identifying future bottlenecks, hidden winners, and emerging champions across four phases of development.

Phase 1 · 2025–2028 · PROBABILITY: HIGH (85%)
The Foundation Firms

India's first ATMP cluster reaches operational scale. Micron's memory packaging at Sanand operates at capacity. CG Semi and Kaynes Semicon ship volumes and qualify for advanced packaging. Tata Electronics-PSMC fab construction advances. ISM 2.0 program grants begin flowing to materials and equipment companies. Key winners: CG Power, Kaynes Technology, Linde India, Inox Air Products, Va Tech Wabag, ABB India. Critical bottleneck that will emerge: Electronic-grade specialty gas supply — India's growing cluster will outpace import capacity, creating a domestic supply premium for first-qualified domestic producers (Linde India most likely beneficiary). Risk: Wafer supply for ATMP depends on Tata fab timeline; standard OSAT oversupply if fab output underwhelms.

Phase 2 · 2028–2033 · PROBABILITY: MEDIUM-HIGH (65%)
The Ecosystem Build-Out

Tata Electronics-PSMC fab reaches target output. India's second fab wave — likely compound semiconductors (SiC for EVs, GaN for 5G/radar) and possibly a second silicon foundry — begins approvals. Domestic materials supply chains for specialty chemicals and gases reach meaningful scale. ISM 2.0 equipment incentives produce first domestic semiconductor equipment components companies. Key winners: Gujarat Fluorochemicals (electronic-grade fluoropolymers qualified), Navin Fluorine (electronic-grade HF), Carborundum Universal (CMP abrasives), ASM Technologies/HHV JV (equipment components), Thermax (cooling/HVAC). Critical bottleneck that will emerge: Qualified precision engineering capacity for semiconductor equipment components — the gap between India's general CNC capabilities and semiconductor-grade tolerances must be bridged. Risk: Chinese semiconductor overcapacity in mature nodes compresses global pricing; India's 28nm foundry economics deteriorate if China's SMIC races to the bottom.

Phase 3 · 2033–2038 · PROBABILITY: MEDIUM (50%)
The Capability Deepening

India's design ecosystem produces 2–3 globally competitive fabless companies in specific domains (automotive ICs, industrial power management, defense ASICs). Advanced packaging capabilities reach global competitive level. First genuinely Indian-designed chips manufactured at Indian fabs. ISM materials programs produce domestic photoresist alternatives (most likely through multinational technology transfer). Key winners: MosChip Technologies (if design wins materialize), Tata Elxsi (if IP transition succeeds), Centum Electronics (defense ASIC exporter), first domestic photoresist company (likely JV). Critical bottleneck that will emerge: Photomask manufacturing — every chip requires a photomask, and India has zero domestic production. A domestic photomask manufacturer, likely established through foreign JV in this phase, will own a genuine monopoly. Risk: Advanced packaging technology gap vs. Taiwan/South Korea persists if investment in heterogeneous integration is insufficient.

Phase 4 · 2038–2045 · PROBABILITY: LOW-MEDIUM (35%)
The Global Competitor

India holds 8–12% of global ATMP capacity. A second or third silicon foundry is operational. India exports chips, design services, and semiconductor materials at global scale. Indian equipment sub-system manufacturers have achieved qualifications at non-Indian fabs. Domestic companies form the backbone of India's defense semiconductor supply chain, displacing imports. Key winners: The beneficiaries of this phase are today's Phase 1–2 winners, compounding. The infrastructure companies (Linde India, Va Tech Wabag, GFL, Navin Fluorine) will have delivered 15–20 years of compounding earnings growth. The equipment companies will have established international customer bases. Most likely creators of long-term shareholder wealth: Electronic gases and materials companies (structural compounders regardless of cycle), advanced packaging leaders (if moat is built), design services companies transitioning to IP ownership. Critical risk: Geopolitical disruption to equipment imports (ASML EUV export restrictions could affect advanced node plans), talent gap if India fails to train sufficient semiconductor engineers (from 125K to 500K required), and infrastructure challenges in power and water that could constrain fab development.

The 5 Most Important Conclusions for Long-Term Investors

1. Own the infrastructure, not the chip company. The businesses supplying ultra-pure water, electronic gases, specialty fluoropolymers, and precision power to India's fabs will compound more reliably and at higher ROIC than the fabs themselves. This is the ASML principle applied to India.

2. The materials gap is the biggest investment opportunity nobody is discussing. India imports essentially 100% of its semiconductor-grade chemicals and materials. ISM 2.0 explicitly targets this. The Indian chemicals companies that achieve semiconductor-grade qualifications in the next 3–5 years will own positions of extraordinary durability. GFL and Navin Fluorine are the most credible candidates.

3. Advanced packaging is more important than the fab. The global architecture of chip manufacturing is shifting toward heterogeneous integration. Advanced packaging — assembling chiplets from different foundries into a single package — is increasingly where semiconductor IP and value resides. India's OSAT players need to invest in 2.5D/3D packaging capabilities now, or risk being relegated to commodity assembly in a world that has moved past wire bonding.

4. ASM Technologies is India's most underappreciated semiconductor investment. The only listed Indian company with actual semiconductor equipment manufacturing capability, through its JV with HHV Group. Currently priced as a mid-size engineering services company with zero premium for its equipment manufacturing position. This is likely the highest-asymmetry investment in India's semiconductor ecosystem.

5. Avoid the EMS narrative trap. Standard electronics manufacturing services — however well-run — are not semiconductor moat businesses. Dixon Technologies and similar EMS providers are excellent companies in their own right but are not the "semiconductor compounders" that their current narrative premiums imply. Distinguish between being part of the electronics industry and owning a structural bottleneck in the semiconductor supply chain.
