Executive Summary

Where Electrons End,

This report presents a comprehensive institutional framework for understanding one of the most consequential technological transitions of the coming two decades — the shift from electron-based to photon-assisted, and ultimately photon-dominant, computing and communications infrastructure.

The Central Thesis

The global computing infrastructure stands at a structural inflection point. For seven decades, the semiconductor industry scaled on the predictable cadence of Moore's Law — doubling transistor density approximately every two years, delivering exponential performance gains at declining cost per transistor. That era is effectively over.

Physical limits — quantum tunneling leakage, electromigration, heat dissipation at nanometre scales, and interconnect propagation delay — have arrested the reliable progression of electrical computing. Simultaneously, artificial intelligence has imposed a demand for compute that grows not linearly but exponentially, with frontier training runs doubling in computational requirement every six to twelve months.

Into this constraint-laden environment, photonics emerges not merely as an incremental technology but as the medium through which the next era of computing will be architected. Light travels at the fundamental speed of the universe through transparent media, consumes no resistive energy, generates negligible heat, and carries information at bandwidth densities physically impossible for copper conductors. The transition from electron-dominant to photon-assisted infrastructure is not theoretical — it is happening in production data centres today, with co-packaged optics, silicon photonic transceivers, and high-speed optical interconnects deployed at hyperscale.

Part One

The Photonics Revolution

Understanding why the world may be approaching the most significant transition in computing infrastructure since the shift from vacuum tubes to transistors — and why photons are the only viable answer to the constraints that electrons now face.

1.1 The Collapse of Dennard Scaling

Robert Dennard's 1974 observation established that as transistors shrink, their power density remains constant — smaller geometries deliver faster operation at the same power per unit area. This elegant property governed semiconductor economics for three decades and is the reason that shrinking chips consistently delivered both higher performance and lower power consumption simultaneously.

Dennard Scaling collapsed around 2006. As gate oxide layers thinned to just a few atomic layers, quantum mechanical tunnelling caused leakage currents that could not be suppressed through further geometric reduction. The consequence was immediate and irreversible: power density began rising as transistors shrank, eventually creating thermal hotspots that physical cooling cannot adequately address at modern node densities.

The industry response — parallelism through multicore architecture, specialised accelerators, chiplet disaggregation, and heterogeneous integration — has been impressive. But each of these responses demands more data movement between compute elements. More data movement over copper wires means more power consumed in signal transmission, more heat generated by resistive conductors, and more bandwidth congestion in the electrical interconnect fabric. The cure has accelerated the disease.

1.2 The AI Compute Explosion

The computational appetite of artificial intelligence has grown in a manner that defies straightforward historical comparison. Frontier training runs — the large-scale computations that produce general-purpose AI models — have increased in required floating-point operations by a factor of approximately 300,000 between 2012 and 2023, while the leading models of 2024–2025 represent a further order-of-magnitude escalation. Research by Epoch AI indicates that compute used in frontier training doubles approximately every 6–12 months.

This demand creates a structural asymmetry: compute capability scales faster than data movement capability. A GPU cluster's raw computational throughput — measured in floating-point operations per second — can scale by stacking more accelerators and improving silicon density. But the bandwidth between those accelerators, the speed at which model weights can be loaded from memory, and the throughput of rack-to-rack and data-centre-to-data-centre interconnects scale far more slowly through purely electrical means.

The result is a systemic bottleneck in which expensive, power-hungry compute units sit idle waiting for data. Every idle cycle represents capital destruction. The resolution of this bottleneck is the primary commercial driver of photonics adoption in AI infrastructure today.

The Bandwidth Wall — Core Insight

NVIDIA's H100 delivers approximately 3,958 TFLOPS of FP8 compute but requires memory bandwidth of 3.35 TB/s through HBM3 — and that HBM3 already represents the frontier of electrical memory interconnect technology. The next generation of models will require memory bandwidth that cannot be delivered through stacked DRAM alone. Optical memory interconnects and photonic chiplet integration are not roadmap abstractions — they are engineering necessities that the industry is actively funding.

1.3 The Power Consumption Crisis

AI data centres are approaching power consumption profiles that challenge existing electrical grid infrastructure in major markets. A single NVIDIA H100 GPU cluster of 1,024 units consumes approximately 3–5 MW of total power including cooling. Hyperscale AI training facilities of 10,000–100,000 accelerators require 50–500 MW — equivalent to the baseline load of a mid-sized city. Global data centre power consumption exceeded 200 TWh in 2023 and is conservatively projected to reach 400–600 TWh by 2030, representing approximately 1.5–2% of global electricity generation.

The critical point for photonics investment is this: electrical interconnects — the wires, vias, PCB traces, and copper cables that carry data between chips, modules, and racks — account for approximately 35–45% of total AI server power consumption. This is not primarily compute power; it is the thermodynamic cost of moving information electrically through resistive conductors. Photons, by contrast, traverse optical fibre with attenuation of approximately 0.2 dB/km and consume energy only at the transmitter and receiver — not along the transmission path.

1947
Transistor Replaces Vacuum Tube
Bell Laboratories' invention of the bipolar transistor initiated the transition from thermionic to solid-state electronics. Vacuum tubes were faster to produce initially, but transistors were smaller, cheaper, more reliable, and far more power-efficient. The transition was complete within 15 years across most computing applications. This transition maps conceptually to photons replacing electrons for data movement — the fundamental physics advantage is similarly decisive.
1970s–1980s
Copper to Optical Fibre in Telecommunications
Corning's 1970 demonstration of low-loss optical fibre (attenuation below 20 dB/km) initiated the transition of long-haul telecommunications from copper cable to glass fibre. By the late 1980s, the US had committed to fibre for all new long-distance infrastructure. The transition created enormous value for fibre manufacturers, connector suppliers, amplifier developers, and systems integrators. Corning, having supplied the enabling material, captured durable economic value across the entire subsequent build-out.
1990s
Circuit Switching to Optical Packet Networking
The internet's explosive growth created demand for optical networking systems that could route data at line rate without electrical conversion at intermediate nodes. Dense Wavelength Division Multiplexing (DWDM) enabled a single fibre to carry hundreds of simultaneous optical channels, each at multi-gigabit rates. Companies providing DWDM systems, optical amplifiers, and wavelength-selective switches captured premium margins through most of the 1990s buildout.
2010s–Present
Electrical Chip-to-Chip Interconnects Approach Limits
As AI accelerator clusters scale, the electrical SerDes (Serialiser/Deserialiser) links that connect chips within and between servers approach physical limits of signal integrity, power consumption, and bandwidth density. Industry consortia (OIF, CXL Consortium, UALink) are standardising optical alternatives. Co-packaged optics — integrating optical transceivers directly into switch and accelerator packages — began production deployment at hyperscale in 2023–2024.
2025–2035
The Photonics Inflection — We Are Here
Silicon photonics, co-packaged optics, glass substrates, and optical computing transition from advanced pilot to mainstream infrastructure. This is the period of maximum commercial opportunity — analogous to 1985–1995 in optical telecommunications. Early entrants and material monopolists will establish positions that compound for decades.

1.4 Why Photons: The Physics Advantage

Photons possess a set of physical properties that make them categorically superior to electrons for data transport across most length scales relevant to AI infrastructure — from chip-to-chip distances of millimetres to data-centre interconnect distances of kilometres:

Zero resistive loss: Photons do not interact with the lattice of a conductor in the way electrons do. Optical fibre attenuation is governed by material purity and Rayleigh scattering, not by resistivity. This means no heat is generated during transmission — the dominant power cost is only at the electro-optical conversion points.

Wavelength multiplexing: Multiple independent optical channels can co-exist on a single waveguide or fibre by using different wavelengths. Dense wavelength division multiplexing (DWDM) enables a single fibre to carry hundreds of independent data streams simultaneously — a form of parallelism with no analogue in electrical wires.

No electromagnetic interference: Optical signals do not radiate or pick up electromagnetic interference, eliminating the signal integrity constraints that dominate high-speed electrical PCB design. This simplifies packaging and allows optical channels to operate at higher frequencies than electrical alternatives.

Latency: Signals propagate through optical fibre at approximately 2×10⁸ m/s (two-thirds of c), compared to approximately 1.5–2×10⁸ m/s in copper conductors — faster, and without the high-frequency skin effect degradation that forces electrical signals to use elaborate equalisation techniques.

Investment Conclusion — Part One

The photonics transition is not a speculative technological bet — it is a physical necessity driven by the intersection of AI compute scaling with electrical interconnect limits. The question for investors is not whether photonics will grow, but which layers of the photonics value chain will capture the most durable economic value, and which companies will occupy the bottleneck positions that confer pricing power and moat depth analogous to ASML in lithography or Corning in optical fibre.

Global Photonics Value Chain

A comprehensive seven-tier mapping of the global photonics ecosystem — from raw optical materials through design software — identifying where bottlenecks exist, where margins concentrate, and which layers represent the most durable competitive positions.

Analytical Framework

The photonics value chain is analysed in seven tiers, progressing from upstream materials through downstream systems. Bottleneck intensity generally increases with material uniqueness and process complexity, not necessarily with proximity to the final system. The most attractive long-term positions are frequently upstream — in materials and core components whose substitution is physically or economically impossible.

Part Three

AI Infrastructure Bottleneck Analysis

Artificial intelligence is not merely a growth driver for photonics — it is the single event that transforms photonics from a niche enabling technology into a mainstream infrastructure category. Understanding the specific structural bottlenecks in AI infrastructure is essential to understanding why photonics demand is not cyclical but structural.

3.1 GPU Scaling & The Interconnect Gap

The relentless scaling of AI accelerator computational throughput has outpaced the scaling of data movement capability. NVIDIA's H100 SXM5 delivers 3,958 TFLOPS in FP8 mode. The Blackwell B200, introduced in 2024, delivers approximately 18 PFLOPS in FP8 — a 4.5× uplift over H100. The forthcoming Rubin architecture targets a further 3–5× improvement.

By contrast, NVLink bandwidth — the high-speed electrical interconnect that binds GPUs within an NVLink domain — scaled from 600 GB/s (H100) to approximately 1.8 TB/s (Blackwell) — a 3× improvement against a 4.5× compute uplift. The ratio of compute to interconnect bandwidth is worsening with each generation. This divergence is the mathematical inevitability that drives co-packaged optics adoption.

The consequence is that AI clusters composed of thousands of accelerators are increasingly bandwidth-bound rather than compute-bound. Model parallelism across racks of GPU servers requires terabits per second of cross-rack bandwidth — currently satisfied by 400G InfiniBand or Ethernet, transitioning to 800G, and heading toward 1.6T. Electrical pluggable transceivers for 1.6T face severe signal integrity, power, and density constraints.

3.2 Memory Bandwidth — The Hidden Bottleneck

High-Bandwidth Memory (HBM) stacked directly alongside GPU compute dies has been the primary tool for delivering the memory bandwidth that AI training requires. HBM3 delivers approximately 3.35 TB/s per H100 module. HBM3e, deployed in Blackwell, delivers approximately 4.8 TB/s. But this scaling has reached diminishing returns — the physics of DRAM (dynamic random access memory) impose hard limits on cell density and array access latency that cannot be overcome through further CMOS node scaling alone.

The nascent solution being developed by industry consortia — including the Compute Express Link (CXL) consortium and JEDEC — is optical memory interconnects that would allow DRAM arrays to be physically separated from compute silicon by optical links, enabling pooled memory architectures where any accelerator can access any memory at optical bandwidth. This is a foundational architectural change with profound implications for the silicon photonics and packaging industries.

Seven AI Infrastructure Bottlenecks That Photonics Addresses

GPU Bandwidth Scaling Gap: Compute scales faster than electrical interconnect. Co-packaged optics provide the bandwidth density and power efficiency to close this gap.
Memory Bandwidth Wall: HBM reaches physical density and latency limits. Optical memory interconnects enable disaggregated, pooled memory at optical bandwidth.
Data Centre Interconnect Congestion: Rack-to-rack bandwidth at 400G is insufficient for large-scale model parallelism. Optical switching and 1.6T coherent links are the only scalable solutions.
Power Consumption Crisis: Electrical transceivers for 800G+ consume 10–20W each. Silicon photonic co-packaged optics can reduce this to 2–5W per port — a 70% power saving at enormous scale.
Thermal Management Limits: Heat from resistive interconnects compounds chip thermal density. Eliminating electrical-optical conversion losses reduces rack-level heat load significantly.
Networking Density Constraints: 51.2 Tbps AI switch chips require more port density than pluggable optics can physically deliver. Co-packaged optics increase port density 4–10× at the switch level.
Latency: For distributed AI inference at scale, optical switching provides near-nanosecond latency versus microsecond-to-millisecond electrical alternatives — critical for emerging real-time AI applications.

3.3 Co-Packaged Optics — The Central Technology

Co-packaged optics (CPO) represents the most important near-term deployment of photonics in AI infrastructure. The concept is architecturally straightforward: instead of a pluggable optical transceiver (a discrete module inserted into a front-panel cage), the optical engine — the laser, modulator, and detector components — is integrated directly into the switch or accelerator package, connected to the switch ASIC via ultra-short electrical traces on the substrate or through silicon photonics integration.

The benefit is enormous: the electrical SerDes link from ASIC to transceiver — which consumes the majority of pluggable transceiver power — is reduced from hundreds of millimetres to millimetres or less. This eliminates the majority of the equalisation, retiming, and amplification circuitry that accounts for most transceiver power consumption. The result is a substantial reduction in total switch power budget and a significant increase in achievable bandwidth density.

Meta's Wedge400C, deployed in production hyperscale infrastructure in 2023, demonstrated CPO at scale with 12.8 Tbps switching capacity. Microsoft and Google have publicly committed to CPO-based switching infrastructure for next-generation AI clusters. NVIDIA's future Spectrum-X and InfiniBand platforms are expected to integrate CPO elements by 2026–2027. The transition from pluggable to co-packaged optics will create substantial demand for silicon photonics components, optical engines, specialty glass substrates, and the laser sources — and will redirect that demand into the package rather than into discrete front-panel modules.

Strategic Investment Insight — Co-Packaged Optics

The transition to co-packaged optics does not merely increase photonics demand — it restructures where in the supply chain photonics value is captured. Pluggable transceiver manufacturers currently capture value in finished modules sold to networking OEMs. In a CPO world, optical engines are designed into the switch package at the silicon level — closer to the semiconductor and substrate supply chains, and further from the transceiver manufacturing ecosystem. Companies that position at the silicon photonics and optical engine layer — rather than in finished pluggable module assembly — are better positioned for the CPO transition.

Investment Ranking — Layer-by-Layer Analysis

Most Attractive — Design Software: Photonic design automation occupies the highest-quality business model position in the entire ecosystem. Software economics — near-zero marginal cost, extreme switching costs once design workflows are established, and a monopoly rent structure analogous to Synopsys and Cadence in EDA — make the dominant PDA platform the long-term prize. The market is currently fragmented, creating a consolidation opportunity of significant magnitude.

Second Tier — Materials and Laser Sources: Optical materials (primarily specialty glass and compound semiconductor substrates) and laser source manufacturers sit at the intersection of irreplaceable process knowledge and genuine supply inelasticity. Demand can grow faster than capacity because the capacity is limited by process expertise and qualified equipment, not simply capital. This creates genuine pricing power — the ability to raise prices as demand grows without proportional competitive response.

Third Tier — Glass Substrates (Emerging): Glass substrates represent the highest near-term investment opportunity on a risk-adjusted basis because the transition from organic to glass substrates is now beginning — qualification cycles are starting at Intel, Samsung, and other leading AI packaging customers, and the window to establish a durable position in this market is measured in 24–36 months. Companies that achieve qualification before 2027 may hold positions analogous to Corning in optical fibre — established first-movers in a market that scales for decades.

Least Attractive — Pluggable Transceiver Assembly: The pluggable transceiver module assembly business — fabricating and packaging optical transceivers from purchased components — represents a structurally challenged model. Assembly operations are capital-intensive, low-margin, increasingly automated, and subject to commoditisation as form-factor standards remove differentiation. The structural shift to co-packaged optics accelerates this dynamic, potentially reducing the market for pluggable transceivers significantly after 2028.

The ASML Framework for Photonics

ASML — the Dutch lithography equipment monopolist — is the canonical example of how a supplier can achieve a position of such mission-criticality that the entire global semiconductor industry depends upon it. Applying the ASML test to photonics companies reveals which firms have achieved analogous positions of irreplaceability.

The ASML Test — Definition

A company receives a high ASML Score if: (1) its absence would immediately impair supply chains or infrastructure deployment; (2) no qualified alternative exists or could be rapidly qualified; (3) its technology depth represents decades of cumulative, non-transferable process knowledge; and (4) the global ecosystem has organised its development roadmaps around this company's product capabilities. A score of 10 implies ASML-equivalent irreplaceability. Scores below 6 indicate substitutable positions with meaningful competitive alternatives.
