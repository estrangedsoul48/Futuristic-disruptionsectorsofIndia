Section 01

Executive Summary &
Central Investment Thesis

Something fundamental is changing in how the world makes medicines. The last decade witnessed an unprecedented acceleration in the outsourcing of pharmaceutical development and manufacturing — not merely for cost arbitrage, but because the science has become too complex, too capital-intensive, and too specialized for any single vertically integrated organization to master alone.

India stands at the centre of this restructuring. For three decades, India was the world's pharmacy in a narrow sense: a supplier of low-cost generic small molecules to populations that could not afford branded originator drugs. That era produced enormous companies — Sun, Cipla, Dr. Reddy's, Lupin — but it was fundamentally a replication business. The formulation, the molecule, the chemistry: all already invented. India's contribution was execution at low cost.

What is now unfolding is categorically different. Global pharmaceutical companies are not merely looking for cheaper manufacturing; they are looking for scientifically credible, regulatory-compliant, mission-critical development partners who can handle complex molecules from early discovery through commercial supply. They want partners who understand biologics, who can manage ADC conjugation, who have the analytical infrastructure to support regulatory submissions in the US, EU, and Japan.

"India is not transitioning from cheap to expensive. It is transitioning from commodity to complexity — and that transition, once underway, creates entirely different economic structures."

This report is structured around a core analytical question: Who in the Indian listed universe captures durable, compounding economics from this transition — and who merely rides the narrative?

The answers are not uniformly optimistic. India's CDMO ecosystem is real, growing, and structurally underpinned by global forces that will not reverse. But the listed equity market has already priced many of these themes aggressively. Valuation dispersion between true platform businesses and commodity manufacturers has narrowed. Several companies carry narratives that meaningfully exceed their operational reality. Investors must discriminate with considerable precision.

The Five Structural Forces

Five forces are simultaneously accelerating India's position in global pharma outsourcing. They operate independently but reinforce each other in ways that create a genuine supercycle dynamic rather than a mere thematic uptick.

Force 1 — Manufacturing Complexity Migration: The pharmaceutical pipeline has decisively shifted toward biologics, peptides, ADCs, and novel modalities. These require manufacturing capabilities that are fundamentally different from traditional small-molecule chemistry. The capital investment to build these capabilities from scratch — validated at regulatory standard — is enormous. Global Big Pharma increasingly outsources to specialists.

Force 2 — China+1 Diversification: The BIOSECURE Act in the United States, combined with accumulated geopolitical anxiety about healthcare supply chain concentration in a single jurisdiction, has made diversification away from Chinese CDMOs a strategic imperative rather than a preference. India is the most credible beneficiary, with established regulatory relationships, English-language scientific talent, and existing manufacturing infrastructure.

Force 3 — Biotech Capital Cycle: The explosion of biotech startups — particularly in the US — has created an enormous pool of drug developers who possess molecular intellectual property but have no manufacturing infrastructure whatsoever. These companies are structurally and permanently dependent on CDMOs for everything from milligram synthesis to commercial API supply. Their growth funds the CDMO growth cycle.

Force 4 — Patent Cliff & Biosimilar Wave: Over $250 billion in branded biologic revenues will face biosimilar competition through 2030. Manufacturing biosimilars requires the same biological manufacturing capabilities as innovator biologics. Indian companies with credible cell culture and fermentation infrastructure have extraordinary near-term opportunity from this biosimilar wave.

Force 5 — India's Own Science Capital Formation: The BioE3 policy, the National Biopharma Mission, and a generation of India-trained scientists returning from global pharma companies is creating genuine domestic science infrastructure. The talent flywheel is beginning to turn in India's favour in ways that were not true a decade ago.

Section 02

Global Pharma Industry
Transformation

The pharmaceutical industry's structural evolution over the past thirty years is best understood not as a linear progression but as a series of overlapping capital cycle shifts, each triggered by a combination of scientific advance, regulatory change, and competitive pressure. Understanding this history is essential for evaluating where India sits — and more importantly, where it could sit — in the global healthcare supply chain.

The Outsourcing Imperative

The origins of the modern CDMO industry lie in two parallel forces of the 1990s: the emergence of biotech startups that possessed drug discovery capability but zero manufacturing infrastructure, and the recognition by Big Pharma that fixed-cost manufacturing assets carried enormous capital risk in an environment of increasing pipeline attrition.

Drug development failure rates have consistently remained above 90% across all clinical stages. The probability of a molecule entering Phase I trials reaching commercial approval is approximately 10–15%. For oncology — now the largest therapeutic area — that probability falls further. Against this backdrop, owning dedicated manufacturing capacity for molecules that will likely never reach commercial production is economically irrational. The asset-light model, whereby development-stage manufacturing is contracted to specialists, was an inevitable outcome.

The math became increasingly compelling as drug development timelines lengthened. A large pharmaceutical company might have 80–120 active pipeline compounds at any given time. Managing dedicated internal manufacturing capacity for every stage of every compound would require a level of capital allocation that would destroy shareholder value. Outsourcing allows variable cost structures matched to pipeline events.

The Biologics Revolution

The second, and more profound, driver of CDMO strategic importance was the rise of biological medicines. Biologics — proteins, antibodies, nucleic acids, and living cells — are not synthesized through chemistry. They are grown through biology: in cell cultures, fermentation vessels, and living organisms. This distinction matters enormously for manufacturing economics.

Chemical synthesis, while technically demanding, is ultimately a reproducible process. Once a synthetic route is established, it can be transferred between manufacturers with effort but without fundamental barriers. Biological manufacturing is categorically different. A monoclonal antibody is produced in a specific cell line, under specific culture conditions, in a specific bioreactor configuration. The manufacturing process is, effectively, part of the product. Changing manufacturers of a biologic is not just an operational challenge — it is a regulatory event requiring clinical bridging studies that can cost hundreds of millions of dollars and take years.

"In biologics manufacturing, the CDMO does not merely produce the drug — in a meaningful sense, the CDMO co-owns the drug. That is the deepest possible form of switching cost."

This reality has profound implications for CDMO economics. Once a biologic molecule is transferred to a manufacturing partner and regulatory submissions are filed incorporating that partner's manufacturing process, the relationship has a stickiness that approaches permanence. The switching cost is not merely financial — it is regulatory, temporal, and scientific. This is why large biologics CDMOs like Lonza and Samsung Biologics trade at premium multiples despite their capital intensity: the relationship annuity is real.

The Semiconductor Foundry Parallel

The analogy that institutional investors most frequently reach for when analysing CDMOs is the semiconductor foundry model — specifically TSMC's evolution from a contract manufacturer to an indispensable strategic infrastructure layer. The parallel has genuine explanatory power, though with important caveats.

TSMC's competitive moat rests on three elements: process technology leadership (the ability to manufacture at ever-smaller nodes), massive capital investment in manufacturing capacity that creates barriers to entry, and a customer model that avoids competing with clients in the design space. Samsung Biologics — South Korea's answer to the TSMC question in biologics manufacturing — has consciously modelled itself on this framework: massive bioreactor capacity (the world's largest single-site biologics manufacturing facility), a commitment to never developing its own proprietary biologics pipeline, and a relentless focus on quality and regulatory compliance.

The caveats are important. Unlike semiconductor manufacturing, where process technology advances are a linear scale problem (smaller transistors = better performance), biologics manufacturing complexity is not unidirectionally advancing. Different modalities — monoclonal antibodies, bispecifics, ADCs, cell therapies — require fundamentally different manufacturing platforms. A world-class mAb manufacturer is not automatically a world-class ADC manufacturer. This means that unlike TSMC's platform breadth, biotech CDMOs must make strategic choices about which modality platforms to invest in.

AI, Drug Discovery, and the CRO-CDMO Convergence

The most important emerging structural force reshaping the CDMO landscape is the convergence of AI-enabled drug discovery with outsourced manufacturing. Historically, drug discovery (conducted internally or via CROs) and drug manufacturing (conducted internally or via CDMOs) were organizationally and strategically distinct. That separation is eroding.

AI drug discovery platforms — Recursion, Insilico Medicine, Exscientia, AbSci — are generating drug candidates at unprecedented speed. These companies are, almost by definition, asset-light: they possess computational capability and molecular intellectual property, but have no manufacturing infrastructure. Their growth creates direct demand for CDMO services beginning at very early stages of development — milligram-scale synthesis for computational hit validation, gram-scale for in vitro studies, kilogram-scale for clinical manufacturing.

The implication for Indian CDMOs with strong medicinal chemistry capability is significant. If the AI drug discovery wave accelerates the generation of novel small molecules requiring complex synthesis, the capacity constraint will not be AI compute — it will be skilled synthetic chemistry execution. Indian companies like Syngene, Neuland, and Sai Life Sciences with deep medicinal chemistry capability are positioned to benefit from this dynamic in ways that are not yet fully reflected in consensus estimates.

Patent Cliff: The $250 Billion Biosimilar Opportunity

The patent expiry profile of large-molecule biologics through 2030 represents the largest single structural opportunity for Indian biologic manufacturers. Drugs including adalimumab (Humira), ustekinumab (Stelara), pembrolizumab (Keytruda), and nivolumab (Opdivo) — collectively representing hundreds of billions in annual revenues — will face biosimilar competition during this window.

Biosimilar manufacturing is not identical to the innovator molecule's manufacturing process — it is an independently developed process that produces a molecule that is highly similar but not identical. It requires the same bioreactor infrastructure, the same analytical capabilities, and the same regulatory sophistication. The companies best positioned to capture biosimilar manufacturing contracts are those with established biologics infrastructure, strong analytical departments, and existing regulatory relationships with USFDA and EMA.

Key Industry Metrics — Global CDMO Landscape

The global CDMO market is estimated at approximately $230–240 billion in 2025, growing at a compound annual rate of approximately 7–9% through 2030. Biologics CDMO is the fastest-growing segment, expanding at 10–13% per annum. The global outsourcing rate across the pharmaceutical value chain now exceeds 35% by value, up from below 25% a decade ago. Biotech startups — predominantly US-based — account for the largest growth cohort of new CDMO customers. The top 20 CDMOs globally capture approximately 55–60% of market value.

Tier 1 — Drug Discovery & Research: The Intellectual Value Creator

The uppermost tier of the pharmaceutical value chain — drug discovery and research — is where intellectual property is created, where the highest scientific talent concentrates, and where the margin structure, properly captured, is most attractive. It is also where India is most underrepresented relative to its talent base, and where the opportunity for structural improvement is greatest.

A CRO providing medicinal chemistry services to a discovery program is not providing a commodity service. The medicinal chemist synthesizing novel analogues around a pharmacophore, optimizing for potency, selectivity, and ADMET properties simultaneously, is performing creative scientific work that directly determines the value of the resulting intellectual property. This work commands premium pricing relative to manufacturing and is far less price-sensitive.

The switching cost dynamics in discovery services are profound. When a pharma company embeds a CRO's scientists in a discovery program — sharing unpublished target hypotheses, compound libraries, biological data — the informational integration creates genuine lock-in. Moving mid-program to a new discovery CRO would require sharing the same confidential information with an unknown partner, losing accumulated scientific context, and absorbing significant time cost. Programmes typically remain with their initial research partners.

India's genuine strength in Tier 1 is medicinal chemistry and synthetic chemistry talent. The IITs, IISc, NIPER system, and private universities generate large numbers of highly capable synthetic chemists. Several Indian companies — most notably Syngene and Sai Life Sciences — have built genuine discovery research partnerships with global pharma that go beyond manpower arbitrage to include strategic scientific collaboration. This is the form of Tier 1 participation that creates durable economic relationships.

Tier 2 — The CDMO Core: Where Economics Are Made

The CDMO core — API manufacturing, process chemistry, and drug product manufacturing — is where most of the revenue in the ecosystem resides and where the economics are most varied. Understanding the internal differentiation within this tier is the central analytical challenge for equity investors.

At the commodity end of Tier 2 sit bulk API manufacturers producing established molecules for generic pharmaceutical markets. Their economics are driven by scale, cost, and regulatory compliance. Margins are under perennial pressure as multiple manufacturers compete on price. Working capital requirements are significant. ROIC is often below the cost of capital in competitive segments.

At the premium end sit complex CDMO manufacturers serving innovator pharmaceutical companies with novel molecules. The distinguishing characteristics are: high molecular complexity requiring specialized chemistry (high-potency APIs, chiral molecules, controlled substances, peptides); stringent quality requirements exceeding standard GMP; developmental risk-sharing with clients; and long-term exclusive relationships. This end of the tier has EBITDA margins of 35–50% and ROIC profiles that significantly exceed the cost of capital.

The structural driver of India's advantage in Tier 2's premium segment is the combination of scientific talent and cost-competitive infrastructure. An HPAPI manufacturing suite built in India — fully compliant with USFDA containment standards — costs approximately 40–50% less to construct than equivalent capacity in the US or Europe, and operates at 30–40% lower cost per year. For a global pharma company evaluating where to place a 20-year commercial manufacturing contract, this cost differential — applied over the contract lifetime — is strategically significant.

Peptides: The Current Supercycle Within the Supercycle

Among all segments of Tier 2, peptides deserve special attention because the demand shock from GLP-1 agonists — semaglutide, tirzepatide, and their successors — has created a supply-demand imbalance that will take the better part of the decade to resolve. GLP-1 agonists are peptides: chains of amino acids synthesized through solid-phase peptide synthesis (SPPS) or solution-phase chemistry. Their manufacturing is chemistry-intensive, scale-sensitive, and requires significant capital investment in specialized purification equipment (HPLC columns, preparative chromatography systems).

Global demand for GLP-1 APIs — driven by the extraordinary commercial success of Ozempic and Wegovy — has outstripped available CDMO capacity by a significant margin. Novo Nordisk and Eli Lilly are simultaneously investing in internal capacity and placing large external CDMO contracts. Indian companies with established peptide manufacturing capabilities — including Divi's Laboratories and Neuland Laboratories — are well-positioned to capture a portion of this demand. The margin environment for peptide API manufacturing, which was already attractive at 35–45% EBITDA, has become exceptional in the current supply-constrained environment.

ADCs: The Next Complexity Frontier

Antibody-drug conjugates represent the most technically demanding segment of the current innovator pharma pipeline. An ADC consists of a monoclonal antibody, a cytotoxic payload (typically a cancer-killing small molecule of extraordinary potency), and a linker chemistry connecting the two. Manufacturing an ADC requires the convergence of biologics manufacturing (the antibody), complex HPAPI synthesis (the payload, often at nanomolar cytotoxicity levels), and conjugation chemistry — all under conditions that require specialized containment and regulatory compliance.

The number of CDMOs globally with credible ADC manufacturing capabilities remains small — perhaps 12–15 organisations worldwide. This supply concentration, combined with a pipeline of 100+ ADC molecules in clinical development, creates a structural demand-supply gap. Indian companies building ADC manufacturing capabilities today — Piramal Pharma being the most advanced — are positioning for a market that will likely be structurally supply-constrained for the next 5–8 years.

Tier 3 — Biologics: The Capital-Intensive High Ground

Biological manufacturing represents the highest capital intensity and, when executed at scale with regulatory approval, the most durable competitive position in the pharmaceutical supply chain. A bioreactor suite capable of mammalian cell culture at commercial scale (10,000–25,000 litre bioreactors) costs $300–600 million to build, validate, and license. Once operational and incorporating a specific molecule's manufacturing process into a regulatory filing, it is effectively irreplaceable for that molecule's supply chain.

India's biologics manufacturing capability is real but concentrated. Biocon Biologics has emerged as the most sophisticated Indian biologics manufacturer, with USFDA-approved manufacturing for insulin, trastuzumab (Herceptin biosimilar), and bevacizumab. Biological E has vaccine manufacturing capability at scale, demonstrated during COVID-19. Wockhardt has biologics capability but limited regulatory track record in developed markets.

The capital intensity of biologics has constrained India's participation relative to South Korea (Samsung Biologics) and China (WuXi Biologics). However, the biosimilar opportunity — which requires the same manufacturing capabilities as innovator biologics but serves a market with established regulatory pathways — provides a more clearly defined commercial runway for Indian capital investment.

Tier 4 — Advanced Therapies: Long-Duration Optionality

Cell therapy, gene therapy, mRNA, and the broader advanced therapy landscape represent the longest-duration options in the biotech ecosystem. They are also the most honest test of scientific and manufacturing seriousness, as opposed to narrative positioning.

Among these modalities, mRNA manufacturing has the clearest near-term commercial trajectory for India, given COVID-19's acceleration of the regulatory and manufacturing learning curve. Biological E, Pune-based Gennova (acquired by Emami), and several other Indian firms have invested in mRNA manufacturing capability. The challenge is that the mRNA therapeutics pipeline beyond vaccines remains nascent, and the commercial mRNA CDMO opportunity is currently limited to a small number of large vaccine programs.

Gene therapy and cell therapy manufacturing — plasmid DNA, viral vector production, and ex vivo cell processing — remain very early-stage in India's capabilities. The capital requirements are substantial, the scientific complexity is extreme, and the quality standards are the most demanding in the pharmaceutical industry. For Indian CDMOs, this is a 2028–2035 opportunity at earliest, contingent on significant talent and capital investment in the interim period.

The Radiopharmaceutical Opportunity — Underappreciated

Radiopharmaceuticals — diagnostic and therapeutic agents incorporating radioisotopes — represent perhaps the most underappreciated segment of the advanced therapy landscape from an Indian equity perspective. The clinical validation of lutetium-177 based therapies (Pluvicto/lutetium PSMA for prostate cancer) has opened a significant commercial opportunity. Radiopharmaceuticals have inherently short shelf lives (hours to days) due to radioactive decay, making global manufacturing hubs economically impractical. Regional manufacturing near patient populations becomes essential. India's large cancer patient population creates a structural domestic market, and Indian companies with radiopharmacy capability could also serve regional Asian markets. This segment deserves significant investor attention that it is not currently receiving.

Section 04

India Policy Architecture &
The Bioeconomy Ambition

The Indian government's policy posture toward biotechnology and pharmaceutical manufacturing has undergone a significant shift since 2020. Where earlier policy was largely reactive — responding to supply chain crises and generic pharma competitiveness concerns — the current framework is explicitly strategic, targeting India's positioning as a global biotechnology innovation hub rather than merely a manufacturing cost arbitrage destination.

The headline ambition is substantial: India's bioeconomy, which generated approximately $130 billion in value in 2024, is targeted to reach $300 billion by 2030 under the government's own projections. Whether this specific number is achievable on that timeline matters less than what it signals: a government that understands the strategic value of the biotechnology sector and is deploying policy instruments — tax incentives, infrastructure investment, regulatory reform, and capital — to support it.

BioE3 Policy — The Strategic Framework

The BioE3 (Biotechnology for Economy, Environment, and Employment) policy, launched in 2023, represents India's most coherent attempt to define a national biotechnology strategy. Its scope is notably broader than the traditional pharmaceutical manufacturing focus: it explicitly encompasses precision fermentation, synthetic biology, agricultural biotechnology, industrial enzymes, and climate-relevant bio-based materials.

For pharmaceutical investors, the most important elements of BioE3 are the infrastructure commitments: biomanufacturing hub development, support for biologics and biosimilar clusters, streamlined regulatory pathways for biotech products, and explicit support for academia-industry collaboration in bioprocess engineering. The policy's success will be measured not by documents but by the quantity of bioreactor capacity commissioned, the number of globally competitive biotech companies created, and the extent to which India captures CDMO contracts that would otherwise flow to China, Singapore, or South Korea.

PLI Schemes — The Manufacturing Incentive Architecture

Production-Linked Incentive (PLI) schemes for pharmaceuticals have provided meaningful stimulus to manufacturing investment, though with more nuanced outcomes than headline numbers suggest. The pharmaceutical PLI scheme allocated ₹15,000 crore ($1.8 billion) across three categories: complex active pharmaceutical ingredients, high-value APIs, and finished drug formulations. The Bulk Drug Parks scheme targeted creation of three dedicated API manufacturing clusters with shared infrastructure.

The outcomes have been mixed. Take-up of PLI benefits has been slower than projected, partly because the investment hurdles required significant upfront capital deployment before benefits flow. Pharmaceutical companies that have invested in PLI-qualifying facilities report that the benefits represent meaningful margin uplift — approximately 5–8 percentage points on qualifying products — but the scheme's design requires careful navigation. For CDMO-oriented businesses, the PLI framework provides genuine support for API facility investment, and several companies have structured capital programs around PLI eligibility.

Regulatory Modernization — The CDSCO Evolution

India's regulatory framework for pharmaceutical manufacturing, overseen by the Central Drugs Standard Control Organisation (CDSCO), has historically been perceived as an impediment rather than an enabler. USFDA and EMA inspections of Indian pharmaceutical facilities have identified systematic quality failures at multiple large companies over the past decade, creating a regulatory credibility deficit that has suppressed India's CDMO uptake among risk-averse global pharma partners.

The regulatory situation has improved materially since 2020. The number of Form 483 observations and warning letters from USFDA targeting Indian facilities has declined. The CDSCO has made meaningful investments in inspector training and the adoption of international harmonization frameworks. New risk-based inspection programs and electronic regulatory submission systems have modernized the regulatory infrastructure. These improvements are not merely cosmetic — they represent genuine reductions in the probability of regulatory disruption for Indian CDMO businesses.

Section 05

China+1 & the Global
Supply Chain Reset

The geopolitical restructuring of pharmaceutical supply chains is the most significant external driver of India's CDMO opportunity over the next decade. To understand its scale and irreversibility, one must first understand the depth of China's current position — and why that depth is precisely what makes global pharma uncomfortable.

How China Built Dominance

China's emergence as the world's dominant API and pharmaceutical ingredient manufacturer over the period 2000–2020 was not accidental. It was the product of deliberate state industrial policy: subsidized energy costs, low-cost land and environmental compliance infrastructure, massive investment in chemical engineering education, and the economic clustering effects of concentrated chemical manufacturing zones in Shandong, Zhejiang, and Jiangsu provinces.

The result is extraordinary supply concentration. China currently accounts for approximately 40% of global API production by volume, and in specific therapeutic categories — antibiotics, cardiovascular molecules, pain management APIs — the concentration exceeds 70–80%. The heparin supply chain crisis of 2008, when contaminated Chinese heparin caused hundreds of deaths globally, was a first warning signal that this concentration carried systemic risk. The COVID-19 pandemic of 2020 was a far more powerful demonstration: when China's manufacturing temporarily contracted, global pharmaceutical supply chains experienced shortages across dozens of essential medicines.

The BIOSECURE Act — The Regulatory Catalyst

The United States BIOSECURE Act, enacted in 2024, represents the most significant pharmaceutical supply chain legislation since the Drug Quality and Security Act. Its central provision prohibits US federal agencies — including the Department of Defense and the Department of Health and Human Services — from contracting with companies that use specific Chinese biotech companies (including BGI Genomics, MGI Tech, Complete Genomics, WuXi AppTec, and WuXi Biologics) in their supply chains.

The Act contains a critical provision: existing contracts have a sunset period (initially 2027, with partial extension provisions), after which affected companies must have established alternative supply chains. This sunset creates a hard deadline for global pharma companies that currently rely on WuXi AppTec and WuXi Biologics — two of the world's largest CDMOs — to identify and qualify alternative manufacturing partners.

"The BIOSECURE Act does not merely create an opportunity for India. It creates a deadline — and pharmaceutical supply chain decisions constrained by regulatory deadlines move at a fundamentally different pace than those driven by preference."

WuXi AppTec's revenue from US customers was approximately $3.5–4 billion per annum prior to the BIOSECURE Act. Even assuming that only 30–40% of this revenue can realistically be redirected to Indian CDMOs in the near term (the remainder going to US domestic capacity, South Korea, and Singapore), the addressable opportunity for India is in the range of $1–1.5 billion in incremental annual revenue — representing 15–20% growth on India's current CDMO revenue base.

The Intellectual Honesty Check

The China+1 narrative is real and the commercial flows are genuine. But several important realities must temper investor enthusiasm about the scale and speed of the opportunity.

First, supply chain qualification in pharmaceutical manufacturing is extraordinarily time-consuming. Qualifying a new API manufacturer requires analytical method transfer, comparative process studies, scale-up runs, stability studies on the new material, and regulatory filings. For a regulated product (requiring USFDA/EMA approval of the manufacturing source), the end-to-end qualification process typically takes 24–36 months and $2–5 million in direct costs per molecule. This means that even with perfect intent to diversify away from China, the physical transfer of manufacturing is a multi-year process that cannot be meaningfully accelerated.

Second, India itself remains dependent on China for significant portions of its raw material supply. Key starting materials for several important APIs — including antibiotics, certain cardiovascular molecules, and vitamin intermediates — are predominantly sourced from Chinese chemical manufacturers. This creates a recursive vulnerability: India's ability to replace China in pharma supply chains is partially constrained by India's own dependence on China for upstream inputs. Backward integration — building domestic supply chains for key starting materials — is a necessary condition for India achieving genuine supply chain independence, and it will take a decade.

Third, not all China+1 flows will go to India. Singapore has made sophisticated arguments for precision biologics manufacturing and advanced therapy manufacturing, backed by excellent infrastructure and English-language talent. South Korea, through Samsung Biologics and Celltrion, has established world-class biologics manufacturing that is already capturing significant China replacement contracts in the biologics space. The competitive set for India includes these very capable peers.

The China+1 opportunity is real and India will capture significant share. Investors should expect, however, that the transition will take 5–10 years to fully materialize in revenue, and that the competition for displaced Chinese CDMO revenue is global, not merely India-vs-China.
Section 06

Capital Cycle &
ROIC Architecture

The capital cycle in pharmaceutical outsourcing is one of the most interesting investment dynamics in the global healthcare sector. It contains the paradox that the businesses with the greatest near-term depressed returns — those making the largest upfront capital investments in complex new manufacturing capabilities — often generate the most durable long-term value creation, precisely because their willingness to absorb investment pain creates barriers that competitors cannot easily replicate.

The CDMO Operating Leverage Curve

Understanding CDMO economics requires understanding capacity utilization as the governing variable of profitability. A CDMO manufacturing facility — whether a peptide synthesis suite, a bioreactor hall, or an ADC conjugation facility — carries a cost structure that is predominantly fixed in the near term. The facility must be maintained, staffed, validated, and licensed regardless of how many batches are processed in a given month. Energy costs, quality control overhead, regulatory compliance, and skilled operator salaries do not flex down when utilization is low.

This fixed-cost structure creates dramatic operating leverage as utilization rises. At 40–50% capacity utilization — the typical level during the ramp phase of a new CDMO facility — EBITDA margins may be 15–20%. At 70–80% utilization, the same facility may generate 35–45% EBITDA margins, because the incremental revenue from additional batches flows through at marginal costs of 20–30% or less. The operating leverage inherent in a well-run CDMO business, as utilization moves from ramp-stage to mature-stage, is extraordinary.

Why Early-Stage Capex Distorts Valuations

Indian CDMO companies are currently in the most capital-intensive phase of their development cycle. Divi's is constructing peptide manufacturing capacity. Syngene is building biology capabilities. Piramal is investing in ADC manufacturing infrastructure. Laurus Labs has made the most dramatic transition — from a predominantly generic API business to an aspiring CDMO/biologics platform — requiring sustained elevated capex.

During this investment phase, near-term ROCE is depressed by definition. New capacity has low initial utilization, high depreciation from fresh capital investment, and elevated interest costs from debt financing. This creates a window during which the underlying value of the business — the quality of the manufacturing platform being built, the regulatory approvals being accumulated, the customer relationships being established — is not reflected in traditional financial metrics.

The historical parallel from global CDMOs is instructive. Lonza in the early 2000s, when it was rapidly building mammalian cell culture capacity, traded at depressed multiples because near-term ROCE was below its cost of capital. Investors who recognized that the capacity being built would generate 25–30% returns once utilized — and who had confidence in Lonza's ability to fill that capacity — made exceptional returns as utilization ramped and financial metrics recovered. Samsung Biologics followed a similar trajectory: early years of loss-making as enormous bioreactor capacity was built; then dramatic profitability as utilization reached operating leverage thresholds.

Which Categories Structurally Compound

The businesses that compound most durably in pharmaceutical outsourcing are those that combine scientific capability with regulatory trust, mission-critical product positioning, and long-term customer relationship lock-in. The businesses that underperform are those that compete primarily on cost without differentiated capability — because cost competition is a strategy that extracts value rather than creating it.

A useful mental model is to categorize CDMO businesses along two axes: scientific differentiation (what can this company do that others cannot?) and mission criticality (what is the cost of this company failing a customer?). Businesses high on both axes — producing complex molecules where regulatory files are integrated and failure means supply disruption for a commercially marketed drug — have the most defensible economic positions. Businesses low on both axes — producing generic APIs where multiple qualified alternatives exist — face perpetual competitive pressure.

The Hidden Economics of Consumables

One of the most systematically underappreciated investment opportunities in India's biotech infrastructure is the laboratory consumables and equipment segment. Companies supplying single-use bioprocess equipment, analytical labware, cell culture consumables, and specialized containers to pharmaceutical and biotech manufacturers operate business models with characteristics that are structurally superior to the CDMO businesses they serve.

Consider the economics: a pharmaceutical company that has qualified Tarsons Products' centrifuge tubes for use in a biologics manufacturing process — incorporating them into validated GMP procedures and stability studies — is not going to switch to an alternative supplier to save 15% on consumables. The validation cost of switching exceeds years of cost savings. This creates precisely the annuity dynamic — recurring, price-insensitive revenue from qualified customers — that makes consumables businesses valuable.

Tarsons Products, the most visible Indian listed company in this segment, benefits from this dynamic. Its product range — PCR consumables, centrifuge tubes, cell culture flasks, storage containers — addresses the fundamental working infrastructure of every biology laboratory in India. As India's biotech manufacturing expands, the demand for high-quality, locally sourced GMP-grade consumables will grow in proportion.

Section 09

Mission-Criticality &
The Invisible Monopolies

The concept of mission-criticality is the most important lens through which to evaluate pharmaceutical outsourcing businesses. A supplier becomes mission-critical when the cost of its failure — in terms of supply disruption, regulatory consequences, or clinical program damage — substantially exceeds the value of its services. When this asymmetry exists, pricing power follows as a natural consequence.

Identifying the Toll Bridge Characteristics

In infrastructure investing, toll bridge businesses are defined by their ability to charge for passage through economically essential conduits — bridges, pipelines, ports — where building alternative infrastructure is prohibitively expensive. The pharmaceutical outsourcing equivalent exists in several distinct forms.

The first form is the validated process monopoly. When a pharmaceutical company has incorporated a specific CDMO's manufacturing process into a regulatory filing — their US Drug Master File, their European ASMF, their BLA or NDA — that CDMO has achieved a form of regulatory lock-in that approaches a legal monopoly for that molecule's supply. The cost of switching to an alternative manufacturer requires regulatory approval, clinical bridging studies if required, and years of elapsed time. The CDMO's supply is not merely commercially convenient — it is legally embedded in the product's regulatory authorization.

The second form is the containment capability monopoly. HPAPI manufacturers with validated containment infrastructure (OEB 4/5 containment systems capable of handling compounds with occupational exposure limits in the nanogram-per-cubic-metre range) represent a global supply that numbers fewer than 20 facilities worldwide. A pharmaceutical company developing a highly potent oncology compound has limited alternatives — and those alternatives are typically in the US, Switzerland, or UK, at dramatically higher cost than an Indian HPAPI facility with equivalent containment validation.

The third form is the modality-specific expertise monopoly. ADC manufacturing requires the simultaneous convergence of biologics capability (the antibody), HPAPI synthesis (the payload), linker chemistry, conjugation equipment (with containment), and integrated analytical validation. The number of organizations globally capable of executing this workflow at commercial GMP standard remains very small. This scarcity is structural — ADC manufacturing involves real technical barriers, not merely capital barriers.

The ASML Principle in Pharma CDMO

ASML, the Dutch manufacturer of extreme ultraviolet lithography machines, has achieved a literal global monopoly in its segment — not because it has prevented competition through legal means, but because the technical complexity of what it does is so extreme that no competitor has been able to replicate it over three decades of trying. The pharma equivalent exists at the intersection of scientific capability, regulatory qualification, and established customer trust. Blue Jet Healthcare's contrast media intermediate position, Divi's process chemistry reputation, and Suven's neuroscience molecule supply relationships each contain elements of this "complexity monopoly" dynamic. These are not formal monopolies — but their practical competitive defensibility approaches it.

Why Compliance Compounds

In pharmaceutical outsourcing, regulatory compliance is not merely a cost of doing business — it is a source of compounding competitive advantage. Each USFDA inspection passed without major observations, each EMA audit completed successfully, each 483 response accepted without escalation to warning letter adds incrementally to the regulatory credibility asset of the inspected facility. This credibility asset is not easily replicated by a competitor that has not accumulated the same regulatory track record.

Global pharmaceutical companies, when selecting CDMO partners for commercially critical molecules, conduct extensive due diligence on regulatory history. A single warning letter — even one that has been subsequently resolved — creates a period of qualification suspension. The cost of that suspension, in terms of lost CDMO contracts not awarded or renewed, can be orders of magnitude larger than the direct regulatory resolution cost. This is why companies like Divi's — with a multi-decade record of USFDA inspection success — command premium pricing and retain customer relationships through pricing cycles that would otherwise create pressure to switch.

Section 10

Global Comparables:
What India Can Become

Studying the trajectories of global CDMO leaders provides the most useful template for understanding how Indian companies can evolve — and what the value creation profile looks like as they do. Four case studies are most instructive: Lonza (the original Western CDMO leader), WuXi AppTec (the China model), Samsung Biologics (the Korean biologics infrastructure play), and Catalent/Thermo Fisher (the consolidation model).

Lonza — The Capability Platform Model

Lonza's transformation from a Swiss specialty chemical company to a global CDMO platform is the archetype that Indian companies should study most carefully. In the early 2000s, Lonza made the strategic decision to invest heavily in mammalian cell culture manufacturing capacity at a time when the biologics pipeline was growing but commercial manufacturing outsourcing was still nascent. It built capacity ahead of demand, absorbed the early-stage utilization penalty, and established itself as the preferred manufacturing partner for early biologics companies.

The economics of that bet, realized over the subsequent decade, were extraordinary. Lonza's EBITDA margins expanded from the mid-teens to 30–35% as utilization ramped. Its ROIC moved from below cost of capital to 20%+. The customer relationships formed in the capacity-building years became permanent supply agreements as molecules progressed to commercial manufacturing. The regulatory files incorporating Lonza's manufacturing process became effectively irrevocable.

The Lonza parallel for Indian CDMOs is most relevant for Biocon Biologics and Laurus Labs in biologics, and for Divi's and Neuland in complex chemistry. The investment thesis in each case requires the same analytical framework: is the capability being built genuinely differentiated? Will demand fill the capacity being created? Is management disciplined enough to absorb the utilization-ramp pain without abandoning the strategy?

WuXi AppTec — The Integrated Platform Playbook

WuXi's evolution from a chemistry CRO to a fully integrated CRDMO platform is the most instructive precedent for Indian CRDMO aspirants. WuXi built its platform by starting in chemistry services — where China's cost advantage was most acute — and systematically adding capability layers upward: biology, formulation, clinical manufacturing, commercial manufacturing, cell therapy, gene therapy. Each capability addition deepened customer integration and increased switching costs.

The strategic insight from WuXi is that the platform model creates value that cannot be achieved through any single service line. A customer who uses WuXi for discovery chemistry, process development, and commercial API manufacturing is not merely buying services — they are sharing intellectual property and program knowledge across the entire drug development lifecycle with a single trusted partner. This integration creates switching costs that would not exist if each service were purchased separately from different providers.

Syngene is India's most credible attempt to replicate elements of the WuXi model. The gap between where Syngene is today and where WuXi was at comparable revenue scale is meaningful but not unbridgeable. The critical inputs — scientific talent, regulatory credibility, capital, and time — are all available to Syngene. The geopolitical discount that WuXi now carries (BIOSECURE Act) represents a structural tailwind that can accelerate Syngene's platform accumulation if management deploys the opportunity correctly.

Samsung Biologics — The Korean Infrastructure Model

Samsung Biologics chose a different model: pure-play biologics manufacturing at a scale that no other CDMO could match. Its Songdo facility in South Korea represents the largest single-site biologics manufacturing complex in the world, with bioreactor capacity measured in hundreds of thousands of litres. The strategy was based on a simple hypothesis: biologics are the future of pharmaceutical innovation, and scale in biologics manufacturing creates TSMC-like competitive advantages.

The Samsung Biologics story demonstrates that capital intensity, if deployed with strategic clarity and regulatory discipline, creates truly defensible competitive positions. No competitor can easily replicate Samsung Biologics' capacity in less than five years. No customer who has incorporated Samsung's manufacturing process into a regulatory filing can easily transfer to a new manufacturer. The capital investment, though enormous ($3–4 billion over the first decade), purchased a position that is effectively unchallengeable in its niche.

The Indian parallel would require a comparable level of capital commitment — likely $1.5–2 billion over 10 years — to build a bioreactor campus capable of competing with Samsung Biologics for large-molecule outsourcing. No Indian company has yet committed to this level of biologics infrastructure investment. If one did — with regulatory credibility, management quality, and sustained capital — the returns, once utilization matured, would likely be exceptional.

Section 11

Risk Framework:
What Could Go Wrong

A research report that does not engage seriously with risks is advocacy, not analysis. The India CDMO and biotech supercycle is real — but it carries a specific set of risks that investors must understand and monitor. Many of these risks are not tail events but recurring features of the pharmaceutical outsourcing business that require continuous management attention.

USFDA Warning Letters / Import Alerts
Regulatory action by the USFDA — warning letters, import alerts, or facility-level bans — represents the most acute tail risk for Indian CDMO businesses. A single warning letter can suspend customer qualification of the facility, delay ongoing regulatory submissions, and cause customer contracts to be suspended or cancelled. The financial impact can be catastrophic: Indian pharma companies have lost $500M+ in market value in single sessions following warning letter announcements. This risk is partially manageable through quality systems investment but cannot be eliminated.
Key Molecule Clinical Failure
CDMO companies with significant revenue concentration in single molecule programs — particularly Suven Pharmaceuticals and aspects of the Sai Life Sciences portfolio — carry meaningful exposure to drug development failure. A Phase III clinical failure in a key molecule can eliminate 15–25% of a CDMO's revenue in a single event. Diversification across molecules, therapeutic areas, and development stages is the natural mitigant, but this takes time to build.
Biotech Funding Cycle Contraction
The CRO and early-stage CDMO businesses are heavily dependent on the health of the biotech funding ecosystem, particularly in the United States. The biotech funding cycle contracted sharply in 2022–23 as rising interest rates reduced risk appetite for pre-commercial biotech. A sustained contraction in biotech funding reduces the pace of new drug program initiations and therefore the demand for discovery research services and clinical manufacturing. Indian CROs with significant exposure to early-stage US biotech are most exposed to this cycle.
Capacity Overbuild & Utilization Disappointment
The CDMO supercycle narrative has attracted significant capital investment simultaneously across multiple Indian companies. If demand ramps more slowly than capacity additions — due to slower-than-expected China+1 transitions, biotech cycle weakness, or manufacturing qualification delays — multiple companies could simultaneously experience utilization disappointments that compress margins across the sector. Near-term ROIC could disappoint expectations built into current valuations.
China Raw Material Dependency
India's pharmaceutical manufacturing remains meaningfully dependent on Chinese suppliers for key starting materials, advanced intermediates, and specialty chemicals. In a geopolitical escalation scenario — or even a trade dispute between India and China — this upstream dependency represents a supply chain vulnerability. Companies with the least backward integration into raw material supply are most exposed.
Pricing Pressure in Biosimilars
The US biosimilar market has developed a pricing dynamic more aggressive than many manufacturers anticipated. As multiple biosimilars for the same reference biologic received USFDA approval (particularly for adalimumab), price competition between biosimilar entrants has compressed realized pricing significantly. This dynamic — multiple capable manufacturers competing on price for biosimilar contracts — limits the upside for biosimilar-heavy business models.
Currency and Pricing Risk
Indian CDMO revenues are predominantly denominated in US dollars and euros, while costs are predominantly in Indian rupees. A sustained strengthening of the rupee against major currencies would reduce rupee-translated revenue and compress margins. This currency exposure is partially hedged by leading companies but represents a persistent earnings volatility factor.
Geopolitical India Risk
India's relationship with the United States — currently the most important source of CDMO demand — is generally stable and improving. However, any diplomatic deterioration, trade dispute, or sanctions-related action against India (however unlikely) would have severe consequences for the CDMO sector, which depends on continued USFDA facility access and the ability to export regulated pharmaceutical products to the US. This is a low-probability, extreme-consequence risk.

The Single Most Important Analytical Variable

Across all these frameworks, the single most important variable to monitor is the trajectory of customer relationship depth. Revenue concentration metrics — while useful — miss the more important qualitative question: are the relationships becoming more integrated over time, or are they merely transactional?

A CDMO that grows revenue 20% per year but whose customer relationships remain arm's-length and price-sensitive is not building durable value. A CDMO that grows revenue 12% per year but is progressively embedding its manufacturing processes into customer regulatory filings, expanding its service scope with each existing customer, and being nominated as the preferred development partner for new molecules is building extraordinary long-duration value.

The operational signals of relationship deepening include: multi-year committed volume agreements (not merely preferred supplier agreements), being nominated as the sole qualified manufacturer in regulatory filings, being embedded in customer discovery programs (not merely receiving development-stage orders), and receiving early-development nominations for new molecular entities in advance of competitive processes.

Section 13

The Final
Investment Verdict

The India CDMO and biotech supercycle is the most structurally sound large investment theme in Indian equities over the next decade. It is underpinned by real science, real global demand, real geopolitical restructuring, and real improvement in Indian manufacturing quality. It is also a theme that has already attracted significant market attention and, in several segments, excessive valuation.

The investor task is to discriminate between the businesses that will compound durably through the full cycle and those that are merely riding the wave. That discrimination requires holding several truths simultaneously.

Layers That Capture the Most Value

Complex chemistry CDMOs with proprietary process IP; discovery-integrated CRDMOs with deep molecular relationships; HPAPI and ADC manufacturers with validated containment capability; lab infrastructure consumables with GMP qualification. These layers earn 30–50% EBITDA margins with 20–30% mature ROIC.

Best-Positioned Indian Companies

Divi's Laboratories (regulatory gold standard + peptide tailwind); Syngene International (only true CRDMO platform); Neuland Laboratories (cleanest strategic pivot); Sai Life Sciences (discovery-led CRDMO model); Blue Jet Healthcare (invisible monopoly in contrast media). These five, at right valuations, represent the highest-conviction expressions of the theme.

Firms With Strongest Moat Durability

Divi's process chemistry relationships (30+ year track record, zero warning letters); Blue Jet contrast media position (near-duopoly, validated with global imaging companies); Syngene BMS dedicated science centre (survived 10+ years of BMS restructuring). These moats are real and durable, not narratively constructed.

Overhyped Segments

Generic API China+1 narrative (real but timeline overstated; margins remain under pressure); broad biosimilar manufacturing without USFDA validation (regulatory credibility takes a decade to build, not a year of announcements); mRNA/cell therapy CDMO positioning by Indian companies (genuine 2030+ opportunity, not near-term revenue).

The Bottlenecks That Matter Most

Supply chain qualification timelines (24–36 months minimum, cannot be accelerated); USFDA analytical chemistry inspector capacity (India inspections are underpowered relative to facility count); backward integration into Chinese starting materials (10-year challenge with no shortcuts); biologics bioreactor capital (requires patient, large-scale commitment).

Business Models That Structurally Compound

Integrated CRDMOs following molecules from discovery through commercial (Syngene model); niche-specialty CDMOs with complex chemistry capability and multi-year supply agreements (Divi's/Neuland model); laboratory consumables with GMP qualification lock-in (Tarsons model). All share: switching cost > commodity price difference.

Hidden Beneficiaries Investors Ignore

Tarsons Products (consumables annuity growing with every new lab in India); Blue Jet Healthcare (contrast media oligopoly, underfollowed); Jubilant Pharmova's radiopharmaceutical segment (theranostic oncology validated, supply constrained, genuine infrastructure barrier). None of these are obvious CDMO plays — which is precisely why they may offer better risk-reward than consensus.

Asymmetric Opportunities Today

Companies investing in ADC manufacturing capability before demand fully materialises (Piramal); Indian CRDMOs with direct BIOSECURE Act displacement potential (Syngene, Sai Life); radiopharmaceutical infrastructure before theranostic pipeline broadens significantly (Jubilant); peptide raw material suppliers if GLP-1 demand remains structurally elevated through 2030+.

The Three Principles of India CDMO Investing

After mapping the full ecosystem, three principles emerge as the governing logic for value capture over the next decade.

Principle 1 — Complexity is the moat. Businesses that do hard things — difficult chemistry, complex biology, demanding regulatory environments — earn superior economics precisely because difficulty is what prevents competition. The relentless effort to move up the complexity curve is not merely a growth strategy; it is the only moat-building strategy in pharmaceutical outsourcing.

Principle 2 — Regulatory trust compounds like interest. Every inspection passed, every successful submission, every customer audit completed without major finding adds to the stock of regulatory credibility that the highest-quality Indian CDMOs have accumulated. This stock is invisible on the balance sheet but represents the most durable competitive asset in the business. Protect it at the expense of short-term margin.

Principle 3 — Relationship depth, not breadth, determines value. A CDMO with 50 customers at arm's-length transactional relationships is worth far less than a CDMO with 15 customers whose manufacturing processes are integrated into those customers' regulatory filings. Count relationships that create switching costs, not relationships that could easily be terminated.

The Next Decade:
A Probabilistic Roadmap

The following scenarios are constructed as probability-weighted outcomes for India's CDMO and biotech sector through 2035. They are not predictions — pharmaceutical industry trajectories are inherently non-linear, subject to regulatory events, geopolitical developments, and scientific breakthroughs that cannot be forecast with precision. They are analytical frameworks for thinking about the range of outcomes and the conditions under which each materialises.

Base Case — 65% Probability

India's CDMO sector grows at approximately 15–18% per annum in revenue terms through 2030, driven by China+1 contract wins in complex small molecules, peptide API expansion for GLP-1 programs, and progressive biosimilar commercialization. India becomes the world's second-largest CDMO geography by value (behind China) by 2032. The top 5 Indian CDMOs each exceed $1 billion in annual revenue. EBITDA margins for top-tier complex CDMOs stabilise in the 32–40% range. The India bioeconomy reaches $180–220 billion by 2030 — below the government's $300 billion target but representing substantial growth.

In this scenario, companies like Divi's, Syngene, and Neuland compound at 14–20% per annum in earnings. The equity market rewards quality with premium multiples, creating excellent but not exceptional total returns for investors who entered at today's valuations. The CDMO narrative is validated but does not dramatically exceed current expectations.

Bull Case — 20% Probability

BIOSECURE Act enforcement is more comprehensive and faster than anticipated, accelerating the displacement of WuXi-dependent manufacturing to India across multiple years simultaneously. India captures 25–30% of displaced Chinese CDMO revenue rather than the 15–20% in the base case. GLP-1 demand remains structurally elevated beyond 2030 as oral formulations and new-generation GLP-1/GIP combinations expand the addressable patient population dramatically. Indian biologics manufacturing achieves a step-change with a major international CDMO partnership or Samsung Biologics-scale Indian investment.

In this scenario, leading Indian CDMOs compound earnings at 22–28% per annum. The equity market re-rates to global CDMO multiples. Total equity returns for quality names entered at 2024–25 valuations approach 20–30% per annum over the decade. India's bioeconomy exceeds $250 billion by 2030.

Bear Case — 15% Probability

A cluster of USFDA warning letters targeting Indian manufacturing facilities — triggered by systemic quality concerns or a specific contamination event — creates a sector-wide regulatory crisis comparable to the 2015–2018 period but more severe. The timing of this crisis, coinciding with elevated valuations, produces dramatic multiple compression. Additionally, global biotech funding remains depressed, slowing the pipeline of new molecules requiring CDMO services. The China+1 transition proves slower than anticipated as global pharma prioritises cost optimization over supply chain diversification.

In this scenario, Indian CDMO equity performance is flat to negative for 2–3 years, creating an exceptional re-entry opportunity for long-term investors but severe near-term pain for investors at peak valuations. Quality businesses recover; commodity businesses face structural challenges from which recovery is slow.

The Enduring Investment Conclusion

Across all three scenarios, the businesses that preserve and create value most durably are those with the deepest scientific capability, the strongest regulatory track records, and the most integrated customer relationships. This conclusion is not scenario-dependent — it holds in the bull case (where quality companies capture disproportionate share of accelerated demand), the base case (where quality companies compound steadily), and the bear case (where quality companies recover fastest and from the lowest quality-related damage).

India's CDMO and biotech supercycle is not a trade. It is an ownership proposition — the opportunity to own shares in the businesses that will supply the world's medicines through an era of extraordinary pharmaceutical innovation, from a geography that is uniquely positioned to deliver that supply at the intersection of scientific capability, cost competitiveness, geopolitical alignment, and regulatory trust.

The question is not whether to participate. The question is at what price, in which specific businesses, and with what patience. Get those three variables right, and the India biotech supercycle will reward investors as generously as the semiconductor foundry supercycle rewarded TSMC investors two decades ago.

The Single Sentence Investment Thesis

India possesses the scientific talent, the regulatory infrastructure, the geopolitical positioning, and the manufacturing cost base to become the world's most important pharmaceutical manufacturing partner over the next 15 years — and the listed equity market provides access to this transition at valuations that, for the highest-quality businesses, are demanding but not irrational for investors with patience and precision.

"The greatest risk in the India biotech supercycle is not that it fails to materialise — it is that investors pay for it twice before it does."
