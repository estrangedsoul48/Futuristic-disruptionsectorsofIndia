Section 01

Executive Summary: The Thesis in Full

Central Thesis India is at the inception of a 15–20 year data centre and AI infrastructure supercycle that will require $200 billion in cumulative capital deployment, create multiple compounding equity franchises, and structurally reshape demand across at least six industrial sectors. The bottleneck is not capital or demand — it is power and execution. Investors who understand where value accrues — and where it is merely transited — will generate outsized returns.

India's data centre market crossed 1.5 GW of operational IT load capacity as of early 2026, valued at approximately $10 billion. The consensus projects this figure to double to $22 billion by 2030. Bullish scenarios anchored in AI-accelerated demand see capacity reaching 8–9 GW by 2032, requiring infrastructure investment of $150–200 billion — a figure that rivals the cumulative capex of India's entire telecom build-out.

The catalyst is unmistakable: hyperscalers have committed $67.5 billion in India-specific investments announced in late 2025 alone — Microsoft ($17.5B), Amazon ($35B), and Google ($15B). These are not projections; they are signed MoUs, broken-ground projects, and government-backed commitments with state-level incentives attached. The Vizag AI Hub — Google's gigawatt-scale facility built in partnership with AdaniConneX and Nxtra — broke ground in April 2026 and represents the single largest foreign technology infrastructure project in Indian history.

The analytical challenge for investors is not whether the cycle exists — it does, with extraordinary conviction — but rather where value accrues within the stack, which business models compound over decades rather than merely benefit for a cycle, and which narratives are outpacing fundamentals. This report addresses all three questions with forensic rigour.

Key Investment Conclusions

Tier 1 — Power & Electrical Equipment: The real bottleneck of India's AI buildout is not compute or capital — it is stable, abundant, and affordable power. Transformers, switchgear, UPS systems, cables, and EPC contractors are the "picks and shovels" of this supercycle. Companies with certified, high-specification product portfolios and long order cycles possess durable pricing power and are structurally undervalued relative to the duration of demand ahead.

Tier 2 — Data Centre Operators: India's colocation market is a structural oligopoly in formation. With vacancy at 4.3% and 90%+ occupancy at tier-1 facilities, operators with scale, power access, and hyperscaler relationships are effectively toll roads on the digital economy. The REIT-like characteristics of long-lease, government-incentivised DC facilities are not yet priced into most Indian equities.

Tier 3 — Cooling & Mission-Critical Systems: The transition from air-cooling to liquid cooling is a secular shift driven by AI rack density escalating from 10–20 kW to 60–100+ kW per rack. Companies with technical certification, thermal engineering expertise, and customer relationships in this space will earn specialist margins — not commodity returns.

Tier 4 — Fiber & Connectivity: Subsea cable landings (Chennai and Vizag now emerging as strategic nodes) and fiber densification across Tier-2 cities will sustain decade-long demand for optical fiber, cable systems, and network integration companies. This segment is less volatile than compute hardware and more defensible than pure-play software.

Section 02

The Global AI Supercycle: Context, Economics & Historical Parallels

Why AI Infrastructure Spending Has Exploded

The global AI infrastructure buildout is not a technology trend — it is a capital cycle of historic magnitude. GPT-4, Gemini, Claude, and their successors established that large language models produce economically valuable outputs. That discovery set off a race among hyperscalers, sovereign governments, and private capital that structurally resembles three prior supercycles: the US railroad boom of the 1870s, the fiber-optic overbuild of 1995–2001, and the electrification of industrial America from 1890–1920.

The critical distinction from the fiber bubble — a cautionary parallel frequently invoked by skeptics — is that AI demand is real, immediate, and monetised. Enterprise AI deployments are generating measurable ROI. Cloud companies are reporting sustained acceleration in revenue from AI-linked workloads. Unlike speculative telecom traffic projections, AI compute consumption can be tracked at the token level.

The AI Compute Economics Stack

Training vs. Inference: Training a frontier model consumes enormous compute once; inference consumes compute perpetually at scale. As inference workloads scale with adoption, the compute requirement becomes persistent and predictable — more like electricity demand than a one-time installation.

Token Economics: Each query to an LLM consumes GPU time. At 1 billion daily active users — a trajectory that leading AI products are approaching — the GPU infrastructure requirement becomes a function of global human cognitive activity. This is not a passing cycle.

Moore's Law Inversion: Model capability has historically scaled faster than cost reduction. The "model efficiency" gains from algorithmic improvements (e.g., distillation, sparse attention) are being absorbed by capability expansion (longer context, multimodal, agentic) — keeping total compute demand on an upward trajectory.

The Sovereign AI Imperative

Perhaps the most structurally important driver — and the one most underestimated by global investors — is sovereign AI. Governments across the US, China, EU, UAE, Saudi Arabia, Japan, South Korea, and India have concluded that AI capability is a dimension of national power, economic competitiveness, and strategic autonomy. This has created a second, parallel procurement cycle that is disconnected from commercial ROI considerations and driven by geopolitical necessity.

India's IndiaAI Mission, approved in March 2024 with ₹10,372 crore ($1.24 billion) in government funding, established a target of 10,000 sovereign GPUs — a target that has since been surpassed nearly four-fold to 38,000 GPUs by 2025. The government is not merely a cheerleader; it is an anchor tenant creating demand certainty that underpins private capital formation.

The Six Structural Tailwinds

1. Data Localisation: The Digital Personal Data Protection (DPDP) Act 2023 and existing RBI/SEBI sectoral mandates for financial data create a non-negotiable floor of domestic compute demand. Indian citizen data on Indian servers is not a policy aspiration — it is law, and compliance requires physical infrastructure investment regardless of global AI spending trends.

2. Cost Competitiveness: India's construction cost of $6–7 million per MW compares favourably to Singapore ($10–13M) and Japan ($14–18M). Land is cheaper. Skilled construction labour is abundant. Permitting, while complex, is improving through state-level DC-specific policies across Maharashtra, Andhra Pradesh, Tamil Nadu, Telangana, Karnataka, and Uttar Pradesh.

3. Renewable Energy Adjacency: India achieved nearly 50% non-fossil power generation capacity by mid-2025, with 184+ GW of renewable capacity installed. Rajasthan, Gujarat, and Tamil Nadu solar resources allow data centres to structure long-term corporate Power Purchase Agreements (PPAs) that hedge energy costs and satisfy ESG requirements of global hyperscaler operators.

4. Subsea Cable Position: Chennai's role as India's primary subsea cable landing hub — with 15+ international cable systems terminating there — is now being augmented by Visakhapatnam's emergence as a new node, anchored by Google's investment in cable landing infrastructure there. This is not replicable by any other location on a 5-year horizon.

5. Digital Public Infrastructure (DPI) Flywheel: UPI, Aadhaar, and DigiLocker have created a digital-native population at a scale no other emerging market approaches. India's 944 million internet users and 491 million social media users are generating data at rates that structurally justify domestic compute infrastructure regardless of AI spending cycles.

6. Sovereign Demand Anchor: The government is simultaneously a regulatory mandate-creator (data localisation), a direct buyer (IndiaAI Mission compute), and an incentive architect (state DC policies, tax holidays). The 2026 Union Budget's 20-year tax holiday for foreign entities providing cloud services from Indian data centres is a landmark structural incentive.

The Bharat-Scale AI Opportunity India's 22 official languages and 780 living languages create a massive, underserved AI localisation opportunity. The IndiaAI Mission's BharatGen initiative — developing a 17B-parameter multilingual AI model — represents exactly the kind of sovereign AI application that drives persistent compute demand: government services, financial inclusion, agricultural advice, and healthcare decision support at Bharat scale.

Tier 2 Deep Dive
Data Centre Operators & Colocation
Toll Road | Structural Oligopoly | Long-Lease Revenue

India's colocation market exhibits the economics of a structural oligopoly in its formation phase. With vacancy rates compressed to 4.3% industry-wide and occupancy at tier-1 operators consistently above 90%, pricing power resides firmly with operators. The hyperscalers — despite their enormous scale and negotiating power — cannot construct their own Indian facilities rapidly enough to meet demand, making them structurally dependent on domestic colocation capacity in the near term.

The rack economics are compelling at scale: a 1 MW colocation facility generates $2–4 million annually in stable recurring revenue against $6–7 million in construction cost. At hyperscale density (100+ MW), operators achieve 15–20% EBITDA margins at stabilised occupancy, with power-pass-through arrangements that insulate them from energy price volatility. Long lease terms of 10–15 years with hyperscalers create revenue visibility rivalling regulated utilities.

The transition to AI racks — which demand 40–100 kW per rack versus the traditional 8–12 kW — is simultaneously a challenge and an opportunity. Operators with AI-ready power infrastructure (dedicated substations, liquid cooling readiness, N+1 redundancy) command meaningfully higher rental rates. Those with legacy air-cooled infrastructure face a capex obligation to upgrade or a growing obsolescence risk.

Key strategic variable: Access to dedicated power. In India's supply-constrained grid, an operator with a 100MW captive renewable supply agreement is structurally advantaged over one dependent on grid power with its attendant unreliability. AdaniConneX's parent-group advantage in both renewable generation and transmission is a genuine moat that is underappreciated in current market discourse.

Tier 3 Deep Dive
Power & Energy Infrastructure
The Real Bottleneck | Compounding Utility | Policy-Protected

Power is not a supporting character in the India AI infrastructure story — it is the protagonist. A single AI training cluster of 1,000 H100 GPUs consumes approximately 1 MW of power, 24 hours per day, 365 days per year. A gigawatt-scale facility like Google's Vizag campus will consume as much power as a mid-sized Indian city. The demand trajectory is staggering: India's data centres consumed approximately 2–3 GW of power in 2025; consensus models project this reaching 15–20 GW by 2030 in an accelerated scenario.

India's Central Electricity Authority has estimated ₹9.15 lakh crore (~$110 billion) in transmission investment will be required over the next decade — a figure that would be dramatically larger if AI demand materialises at the upper end of projections. Power Grid Corporation of India, as the dominant transmission utility, sits at the intersection of renewable energy buildout (generation mostly in Rajasthan, Gujarat, Tamil Nadu) and consumption (concentrated in Maharashtra, Karnataka, Andhra Pradesh, Delhi). Every unit of renewable power flowing to a data centre passes through Power Grid's wires.

The transformer shortage — already acute globally and in India — is a critical near-term constraint. Lead times for large power transformers of 160 MVA and above have extended to 24–36 months in India, mirroring global shortages. This creates durable pricing power for domestic manufacturers with established certifications and customer relationships.

Tier 4 Deep Dive
Electrical & Mission-Critical Equipment
Hidden Monopolies | Engineering Complexity Moat | Long Replacement Cycles

The electrical equipment layer — switchgear, transformers, UPS systems, backup generators, precision air distribution units — is the most overlooked value layer in the entire ecosystem. These components are mission-critical (a failed transformer or UPS can destroy $100M in customer equipment and trigger penalty clauses), technically complex (requiring BIS/IEC certifications, hyperscaler vendor approvals, and precision engineering), and characterised by long replacement cycles and high switching costs.

ABB India and Siemens India — both subsidiaries of global technology leaders with full product stacks and certification portfolios — are the clearest structural beneficiaries. Their advantage is not merely product breadth but the accumulated trust and vendor approval that hyperscalers require before allowing electrical equipment into mission-critical facilities. A new entrant cannot replicate this certification history in under 3–5 years.

The genset market, dominated by Cummins India, is experiencing a structural uplift from two compounding sources: AI data centres require N+1 or 2N diesel backup capacity (every 1 MW of IT load requires 1.5–2 MW of genset backup); and the transition of grid power to renewable sources increases intermittency, making backup generation more critical, not less. Cummins India's market leadership, product range, and established service network create a near-impregnable position.

Tier 5 Deep Dive
Cables, Fiber & Connectivity Infrastructure
Recurring Infrastructure Demand | Essential Network Component | Geographic Expansion

India's fiber densification story is decades long and has decades left. BharatNet — the government's rural fiber connectivity initiative — is laying the groundwork for a digital infrastructure layer across 600,000+ villages. AI-driven applications in agriculture, health, and education at Bharat scale require this last-mile connectivity. This is a government-funded, non-cyclical demand pool that runs in parallel to hyperscaler DC connectivity demand.

High-voltage power cables for data centre campuses, optical fiber for network backbone, and specialised low-loss cables for AI server interconnects all represent structurally growing demand for India's cable and wire manufacturers. The key distinction between commodity wire manufacturers and value-adding cable companies is technical specification: hyperscale data centres require cables with specific impedance, fire resistance, and bend tolerance characteristics that not all manufacturers can produce at required certification levels.

Sterlite Technologies (STL) has a unique position: it is the only Indian company with significant global scale in fiber optic manufacturing, subsea cable components, and network integration, while simultaneously having exposure to BharatNet, private telecom rollout, and data centre connectivity. The company is navigating a post-5G inventory correction but the structural demand trajectory is intact.

The Cabinet-approved IndiaAI Mission (March 2024) with ₹10,372 crore represents the most significant sovereign AI infrastructure commitment in India's history. Its seven pillars — Compute Capacity, Innovation Centre, Datasets Platform, Application Development, FutureSkills, Safe & Trusted AI, and Startup Financing — address every layer of the AI stack from raw compute to governance.

Crucially, the mission has already materially exceeded its initial targets. The original 10,000 GPU compute capacity goal has grown to 38,000 GPUs as of 2025, with the government offering subsidised access at up to 40% reduced cost to Indian researchers, startups, and institutions. The practical effect is that India now has a meaningful domestic sovereign compute pool — reducing dependence on foreign cloud for sensitive AI workloads.

State-Level Competition: A Virtuous Race to the Bottom (for DC Operators)

Rarely in Indian industrial history have states competed so aggressively on fiscal incentives, land allocation, and regulatory fast-tracking as they are today for data centre investments. Andhra Pradesh's Data Centre Policy 4.0 (2024–29) explicitly targets an additional 200 MW and offers land allotment support. Maharashtra has offered dedicated power infrastructure for DC parks. Tamil Nadu has positioned Chennai as a global data gateway. This inter-state competition creates a structurally favourable environment for DC operators over the next decade — the government acts as a cost subsidy mechanism, reducing operator risk.

Policy Execution Risk Assessment The delta between announced policy and executed policy is India's perennial challenge. The critical variables are: (1) grid interconnection — transmission lags remain a genuine bottleneck even as generation capacity grows; (2) permitting consistency across state borders; and (3) the 20-year tax holiday's implementation rules, which could meaningfully affect foreign operator economics. Investors must distinguish between policy announcement risk and delivery execution risk.

Capital Cycle Analysis: Who Captures Value, Who Earns Commodity Returns

The Capital Cycle Theorem In any infrastructure supercycle, the highest returns accrue to businesses that combine (a) structural demand growth, (b) constrained supply of the specific asset or capability, and (c) durable switching costs or technical certification barriers. The most dangerous investments are those with high demand growth but unconstrained supply — these exhibit strong revenue growth but collapsing unit economics.

Which Layers Will Suffer Commoditisation

Data Centre Construction (EPC Layer): As more domestic contractors gain experience and international players establish Indian operations, construction margins will compress. The EPC market will bifurcate between companies with proven hyperscale-standard execution (L&T, a small number of specialists) and undifferentiated contractors who will face aggressive pricing competition. The former will sustain margins; the latter will be squeezed.

Generic Colocation: Sub-50 MW, non-AI-ready colocation facilities built without dedicated power and cooling for AI rack density will face obsolescence within 5–7 years. The capital cost of retrofitting is often non-economic relative to building new. Investors should distinguish between AI-ready and legacy colocation assets.

Server Hardware Assembly: Dixon Technologies and similar electronics manufacturers face a nuanced position. Government PLI incentives make them competitively positioned domestically, but the absence of semiconductor manufacturing capability means they remain dependent on global chip supply and subject to margin pressure from import costs and currency movements.

Which Layers Will Become Oligopolies

Large Power Transformers: India has only 4–6 manufacturers capable of producing 400 kV and above transformers at the quality and scale required for hyperscale DC applications. With lead times extending to 24–36 months, these manufacturers — Voltamp, Hitachi Energy India, CG Power, ABB India — are in structural pricing power positions for the foreseeable future.

Precision Cooling for AI: Liquid cooling systems for AI racks require specialised engineering, certification, and customer integration. Blue Star and Voltas have established industrial cooling capabilities, but the liquid cooling for AI is a distinct technology requiring significant R&D. The company that establishes itself as the certified provider for hyperscalers in this segment will earn specialist margins for a decade.

The Energy, Water & Cooling Crisis: AI's Physical Constraint

The Physics of AI Infrastructure

An NVIDIA H100 GPU consumes approximately 700W of power. A rack of 8 H100s consumes ~5.6 kW. A standard AI cluster of 1,024 H100s consumes ~720 kW — nearly 1 MW. A hyperscale AI training facility running 100,000 H100s at full utilisation consumes ~70 MW continuously, equivalent to the power consumption of a city of 100,000 people. These are not projections — these are physics.

The Nvidia Blackwell architecture (GB200), with its 1,000W per GPU TDP, intensifies this further. Next-generation AI racks designed for GB200 NVL72 systems require 120+ kW per rack — more than 10x conventional compute rack density. This creates a fundamental infrastructure architecture challenge that neither air cooling nor conventional data centre design can address.

The Liquid Cooling Imperative

The transition from air cooling to liquid cooling is not a technology choice — it is a physical necessity for AI at scale. Traditional Computer Room Air Conditioning (CRAC) units cannot physically remove the heat generated by GPU-dense AI racks without creating prohibitive hot-spot risks and unacceptably high Power Usage Effectiveness (PUE) ratios.

Three liquid cooling approaches are emerging in India's market:

Direct Liquid Cooling (DLC): Liquid flows directly over heat-generating components through cold plates. Most efficient; requires hardware specifically designed for liquid cooling (NVIDIA HGX architecture supports this). Preferred for training clusters.

Immersion Cooling: Servers are submerged in dielectric fluid baths. Highest thermal efficiency; lowest PUE possible; but highest upfront cost and limited vendor support ecosystem in India. A 3–5 year technology horizon for mainstream adoption.

Rear-Door Heat Exchangers: A transitional technology applicable to existing air-cooled facilities; limited effectiveness at extreme GPU density. Bridge technology only.

Water Scarcity: An Underappreciated Risk

Data centres using evaporative cooling (cooling towers) consume enormous quantities of water — a typical 100 MW facility can consume 2–4 million litres per day. In water-stressed geographies like Chennai, Hyderabad, and parts of Maharashtra, this creates both regulatory and reputational risk. Operators building AI facilities in India must address water consumption proactively, through closed-loop cooling systems, water recycling, or air-cooled condensers — each carrying capital and efficiency trade-offs.

Hyperscalers have made public commitments to water-positive operations by 2030 globally. Microsoft, Google, and Amazon all have Water Replenishment commitments that require Indian facility operators to offset water consumption through local watershed programs. This creates a compliance cost — and an opportunity for specialist water management and treatment companies, including Thermax's water treatment division and Ion Exchange India.

Grid Reliability: The Critical Near-Term Bottleneck

India's grid reliability — measured by grid frequency deviation, unplanned outages, and voltage stability — remains materially below the standards required by hyperscale data centre operations. A typical enterprise data centre requires 99.999% uptime; a single 10-minute outage per year exceeds this budget. In India's grid reality, this means every serious DC operation must be designed for grid independence with substantial on-site generation and storage.

The investment implications are clear: every MW of DC capacity requires 1.5–2 MW of diesel backup generation (Cummins India), 0.5–1 MW of UPS capacity (ABB, Siemens), and increasingly, battery storage (a market where domestic players are emerging). This embedded equipment market is structurally larger than most investors appreciate — for every $6–7M spent on DC construction per MW, an additional $2–3M is spent on power backup and quality infrastructure.

The Valuation Opportunity Window The US electrical equipment re-rating (2022–2025) saw companies like Vertiv trade from 10x to 40x EV/EBITDA as the AI infrastructure thesis became mainstream. Indian equivalents — ABB India, Cummins India, Hitachi Energy India — have begun to re-rate but are likely still in the early innings of a multi-year valuation expansion, assuming the underlying earnings delivery matches the structural demand thesis.

Risk Analysis: The Complete Bear Case Framework

Critical Risk
AI Demand Disappointment — The CapEx Bubble Risk

If AI revenue monetisation at hyperscalers materially disappoints — through slower enterprise adoption, competitive AI commoditisation, or regulatory intervention — the $67.5B hyperscaler capex committed to India could be delayed or cancelled. Historical precedent (telecom fiber 2000, cloud overbuild 2022) suggests demand can be overestimated. The mitigation: unlike fiber, AI demand is already monetised and growing. But the magnitude of capex relative to current India DC revenue creates a vulnerability to any demand deceleration.

Critical Risk
Power Grid Bottleneck — Execution Failure

The single most important risk to India's DC timeline is the grid's inability to deliver stable, high-quality power at scale to DC corridors. Transmission infrastructure lags are a structural Indian challenge. If the ₹9+ lakh crore transmission investment does not materialise on schedule, data centre operators will face power availability constraints that delay deliveries, increase costs, and compress returns. This risk is material and underappreciated by many India DC bulls.

High Risk
Technology Obsolescence — AI Efficiency Gains

Algorithmic efficiency improvements (smaller models achieving similar capabilities, quantisation, distillation) could reduce the compute required per AI inference significantly. If a next-generation open-source model achieves GPT-4 performance at 10% of the compute cost, the GPU demand trajectory could flatten. The risk is real but historically has been offset by Jevons Paradox — cheaper compute enables more applications, sustaining total demand. Investors should monitor model efficiency trends carefully.

High Risk
Geopolitical & Semiconductor Dependency

India's AI infrastructure is almost entirely dependent on imported GPUs — primarily NVIDIA products. US export control policy (already restricting China's access to advanced AI chips) could evolve to impose restrictions on India for geopolitical reasons, or simply through export administration misclassification. Additionally, GPU supply chain disruptions could create prolonged shortages that delay data centre fill-up timelines.

High Risk
Overcapacity in Generic Colocation

The announced $60–70 billion pipeline is substantially larger than demonstrated historical absorption. If hyperscaler demand grows at a 20% CAGR but supply is added at 30%+ per year (driven by investor enthusiasm), vacancy rates will rise, pricing will compress, and returns for undifferentiated operators will disappoint. The market will not be uniform: AI-ready, hyperscaler-contracted facilities will remain tight; generic enterprise colocation could see pressure.

Medium Risk
Regulatory & Data Localisation Policy Reversal

India's data localisation mandates are the key domestic demand floor for DC investment. Any reversal, exemption, or non-enforcement of the DPDP Act and sectoral data localisation requirements (RBI, SEBI, IRDAI) would reduce the mandatory domestic compute demand. The political economy strongly favours maintaining localisation — it is a sovereignty and employment argument — but regulatory uncertainty is real.

Medium Risk
Valuation Compression Risk in "AI Premium" Stocks

Several Indian electrical equipment and capital goods companies have re-rated significantly in anticipation of the AI infrastructure buildout. If order book execution disappoints, margins compress, or the cycle takes longer to materialise than consensus expects, valuations built on peak-cycle multiples face meaningful downside. Investors must stress-test the order book quality and conversion timeline, not just the narrative.

Medium Risk
Water & Environmental Compliance

India's water stress in DC-heavy regions (Maharashtra, Tamil Nadu, Telangana) creates an environmental compliance burden that could increase operating costs or trigger regulatory restrictions. Green Building certifications, water recycling mandates, and future environmental levies could compress DC operating margins by 100–200 bps. This risk is manageable but underweighted in current modelling.

When a hyperscaler is running $100 million in GPU infrastructure 24 hours a day, the cost of downtime dwarfs the cost of premium-priced components. A transformer failure can destroy $50 million in server equipment in seconds. A UPS failure at a critical moment can corrupt a training run worth millions in compute cost. In this environment, reliability is infinitely more valuable than cost.

The Final Investment Verdict

Institutional Conclusion — May 2026
India's AI Infrastructure Supercycle is Real, Structural, and in its Early Innings — But Value Accrues to Infrastructure Owners, Not Narrative Beneficiaries

The evidence is unambiguous: $126 billion in committed capital, ground broken on Asia's largest AI hub, a sovereign government accelerating from 10,000 to 38,000 GPUs in twelve months, and vacancy rates below 5% in a market growing at 30%+ annually. This is a genuine supercycle — and it is structurally more durable than India's telecom boom because it is anchored in data localisation law, renewable energy transition, and global hyperscaler strategy, not speculative subscriber projections.

The investment question, however, is not whether the cycle exists — it is which listed Indian equities will compound capital at superior rates over the next decade. The answer is clear: the power infrastructure layer will deliver the most durable, least volatile, and most underestimated returns.

Layer That Captures Most Value

Power and electrical equipment — transformers, switchgear, UPS, backup power — because these components are mission-critical, technically certified, in structural short supply, and subject to 24–36 month lead times that insulate pricing power from competitive pressure for the foreseeable future.

Best Long-Term Compounders

ABB India, Cummins India, Hitachi Energy India — all combine structural demand growth, narrow competitive fields, hyperscaler certification advantages, and recurring service revenue streams that create earnings floor even in down-cycles. These are 10-year holds, not trading positions.

Asymmetric Opportunities

STL (Sterlite Technologies) — an under-owned fiber infrastructure company mid-recovery from a post-5G cycle correction, sitting at the intersection of BharatNet demand, AI connectivity infrastructure, and subsea cable expansion. Risk-adjusted, this may offer the best reward potential relative to current valuation.

Temporary Beneficiaries

Undifferentiated EPC contractors, generic colocation operators without AI-ready facilities, commodity cable manufacturers without hyperscaler certifications. These will see strong revenue in the build phase but will struggle to sustain elevated multiples as competition intensifies and the market distinguishes quality from volume.

Most Underestimated Sector

Precision industrial cooling — Blue Star, Voltas, and emerging liquid cooling specialists. The transition from air-cooled to liquid-cooled AI racks will drive a wave of retrofit and new-build cooling contracts over 2025–2030 that is materially larger than consensus estimates because it is physically required, not discretionary.

Highest-Risk Narratives

Software-layer AI plays lacking differentiated IP, small-cap "AI infrastructure" companies dependent on a single government contract, and late-entrant colocation operators building generic capacity into an increasingly bifurcated market where AI-ready assets command premium pricing and legacy assets face obsolescence.
